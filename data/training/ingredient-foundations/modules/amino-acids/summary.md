## Summary

This module builds amino acids up from first principles. An amino acid is an organic molecule with an amine group, a
carboxyl group and a side chain that is unique to each one; there are 20 standard amino acids. Linked by peptide bonds
they form peptides (2 to 50 amino acids) and, as longer folded chains, proteins, which the deck explains through the
four levels of protein structure.

It then sorts amino acids into essential, conditionally essential and non-essential groups, picks out the branched-chain
amino acids, and shows how they can also be grouped by charge and polarity. The second half is about use: synergies
with minerals (chelates such as magnesium bisglycinate), applications in animal feed, pet food, baking, flavour,
fortification, sports nutrition, nutricosmetics and dietary supplements, and the formulation issues that decide whether
a product works: heat, pH and oxidation stability, absorption, and the choice between free-form amino acids, peptides
and intact proteins.

It ends with plant-based blends and their limits. Combining pea and rice protein completes the amino acid profile, but a
blend of single amino acids has no peptide structure, so it cannot copy what hydrolysed collagen peptides do. That
distinction is what a colleague needs when customers ask about vegan alternatives.

## Key learnings

- Amino acids are organic molecules with an amine group, a carboxyl group and a unique side chain; there are 20 standard amino acids (slide 4).
- Amino acids are single units, peptides are chains of 2 to 50, and proteins are longer chains (generally over 50) folded into functional structures (slide 7).
- Nine amino acids are essential, including the three BCAAs leucine, isoleucine and valine; the deck lists others as conditionally non-essential (e.g. arginine, glutamine) or non-essential (slide 13).
- Amino acids degrade with heat, pH and oxidation (sulfur amino acids such as methionine and cysteine are most vulnerable); microencapsulation, freeze-drying and liposomal encapsulation protect them (slide 29).
- Free-form amino acids absorb fastest, peptides balance speed and sustained release, and intact proteins absorb slowest; amino acids can compete for the same absorption pathways (slide 29).
- Plant blends such as pea plus rice protein complete the amino acid profile, but single amino acid blends lack the peptide functionality of hydrolysed collagen (slide 30) (slide 31).

## In practice

- When a customer asks for a vegan collagen, explain the deck's point: a plant amino acid blend can match collagen's profile but not its peptides, so it cannot mimic their structure or bioactivity (slide 31).
- Match the form to the product: free-form for rapid absorption in sports and recovery products, peptides such as collagen peptides for balance, intact proteins for sustained release (slide 29).
- Raise stability early: high heat can degrade glutamine and lysine, glutamine degrades in acidic environments, and many amino acids are hygroscopic, which affects shelf life (slide 29).
- Chelation (e.g. magnesium bisglycinate) is the deck's example of amino acids improving mineral absorption and stability (slide 18).
- My reading: the feed, pet food and baking slides (20 to 25) are context on where the bulk of amino acid demand sits rather than our own markets.

## Check your understanding

- Q: What three parts make up an amino acid?
  A: An amine group (-NH2), a carboxyl group (-COOH) and a side chain (R-group) unique to each amino acid (slide 4).
- Q: How does the deck distinguish peptides from proteins?
  A: Peptides are chains of 2 to 50 amino acids; proteins are long chains, generally over 50, folded into functional structures (slide 7).
- Q: Which three essential amino acids are the BCAAs?
  A: Leucine, isoleucine and valine (slide 13).
- Q: Why can two amino acids taken together in large amounts reduce each other's bioavailability?
  A: They often compete for the same absorption pathways, for example lysine and arginine (slide 29).
- Q: What is the main limitation of a plant-based collagen analogue?
  A: It is a blend of individual amino acids with no peptide functionality, so it cannot mimic collagen's peptides or their structural support (slide 31).
