## Slide 4

Source: speaker notes

Amino acids are often called the building blocks of life because they are fundamental to almost every biological process in the body.

At their core, amino acids are organic molecules with three key components: an amine group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), and a side chain or R-group that is unique to each amino acid.

It’s this side chain that determines the specific properties and functions of each amino acid.

Amino acids serve as the foundation for two critical biological structures: peptides and proteins.

When two or more amino acids link together, they form peptides. When these peptides grow into longer chains—50 or more amino acids—they become proteins, which fold into complex structures to carry out a wide range of functions in the body.

These functions include building and repairing tissues, acting as enzymes and hormones, and even supporting the immune system. Without amino acids, processes like muscle repair, energy production, and neurotransmitter synthesis simply wouldn’t happen.

Importantly, amino acids are classified based on how we obtain them. Some are essential, meaning we must get them from food or supplements because the body can’t produce them. Others are non-essential, which the body can produce on its own. There’s also a category called conditional amino acids, which are usually non-essential but become essential in times of illness or stress.

Understanding these basic properties is the foundation for exploring how amino acids are used in nutrition, health, and various industries.

## Slide 7

Source: speaker notes

To fully understand amino acids, it’s important to see how they relate to peptides and proteins.

Amino acids are the smallest structural units in this hierarchy, but when they link together, they form peptides—chains of 2 to 50 amino acids held together by peptide bonds.

These peptide bonds are formed through a process called dehydration synthesis, where a molecule of water is removed as the amino acids join together.

When these peptide chains grow larger, typically containing more than 50 amino acids, they fold into complex three-dimensional structures to form proteins.

Proteins are the workhorses of the body, responsible for countless vital functions, including acting as enzymes, transporting molecules, and providing structural support in tissues like muscles and skin.

The differences between amino acids, peptides, and proteins largely come down to size and complexity: Amino acids are individual units. Peptides are shorter chains of amino acids. Proteins are long, folded chains with specific shapes that determine their functions.

It's also important to note that the structure of any protein - and therefore its function - is defined by its amino acid and peptide structure, and that variations of these can give us truly unique protein forms and applications, such as: Linear proteins like collagen provide structure to skin and connective tissue. Globular proteins like hemoglobin transport oxygen in the blood. Enzymatic proteins catalyze reactions critical for life.

This hierarchy—amino acids to peptides to proteins—is fundamental to how the body works. Proteins are responsible for tasks as diverse as building muscle, repairing tissues, and transporting molecules. Understanding this relationship provides a foundation for exploring their applications in health and nutrition.

## Slide 10

Source: speaker notes

Proteins are organized into four levels of structure, each adding complexity and defining function. The primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acids, which sets the foundation for the protein’s identity. This sequence folds into secondary structures, like alpha helices or beta sheets, stabilized by hydrogen bonds.

Next, the tertiary structure forms when the chain folds into a specific 3D shape. This is where proteins take on their functional forms, like enzymes or hormones. Finally, some proteins form quaternary structures, where multiple folded chains combine into complexes, such as hemoglobin for oxygen transport or collagen in connective tissues.

These structural levels define the protein’s role—whether it’s a globular enzyme driving a chemical reaction or a fibrous protein providing strength to muscles or skin. This hierarchical design makes proteins incredibly versatile, supporting diverse industries like nutrition, beauty, and medicine.

## Slide 13

Source: speaker notes

Amino acids are categorized based on how the body obtains and uses them, as well as their chemical properties.

The first grouping is essential amino acids (EAAs), which must be obtained through diet or supplementation because the body cannot produce them.

These include leucine, isoleucine, and valine—known as branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)—which are key for muscle repair and energy.

Non-essential amino acids, like alanine and aspartic acid, are produced by the body and support basic metabolic functions.

There are also conditional amino acids. Conditionally essential amino acids are a unique group. Under normal circumstances, the body can produce them in sufficient amounts, so they’re classified as non-essential. However, in certain situations—like illness, stress, or intense physical activity—the body’s demand for these amino acids increases beyond what it can synthesize. This is when they become ‘conditionally essential’ and must be obtained from diet or supplements.

For example, glutamine is important for gut health and immune function. During periods of illness or injury, the body’s need for glutamine increases, making dietary intake critical. Similarly, arginine plays a role in wound healing and nitric oxide production, which supports blood flow. During stress or trauma, the body cannot produce enough to meet its needs.

This concept is especially important in medical nutrition and sports recovery. Products targeting gut health, immune support, or post-exercise recovery often include these amino acids to meet the body’s heightened demands.

## Slide 15

Source: speaker notes

I won't focus too much on this, because a video earlier started to explain it quite well, but we can additionally classify amino acids by their chemical properties, such as polarity, charge, or structural groupings.

For example, some amino acids are hydrophobic, like leucine, while others are hydrophilic, like serine, affecting how they interact in proteins and the body.

Building on this, we can also classify amino acids by their charge and acidity. For instance, acidic amino acids, like aspartic acid and glutamic acid, have negatively charged side chains, while basic amino acids, like lysine and arginine, carry a positive charge. These properties influence how amino acids behave in different environments, such as binding to other molecules or stabilizing protein structures.

Another grouping considers the structure of the side chains. Aromatic amino acids, like phenylalanine and tyrosine, contain ring structures, making them critical in protein signaling and interactions.

These chemical distinctions are key when designing formulations or targeting specific physiological functions. Understanding these categories allows us to design products tailored to specific health and performance needs.

Another functional aspect is how amino acids influence protein synthesis. ## Slide 29

Source: speaker notes

Stability and bioavailability are critical factors when it comes to amino acids. Stability refers to how well an amino acid retains its structure and function during storage, processing, and digestion. Some amino acids, like glutamine, are prone to degradation in high heat or acidic environments, which can reduce their effectiveness in formulations.

Bioavailability, on the other hand, measures how efficiently the body absorbs and uses an amino acid. Free-form amino acids, which don’t need to be digested into smaller units, are highly bioavailable and are often used in supplements for rapid absorption. However, amino acids in complex proteins may require longer digestion, impacting the speed at which they become available to the body.

To address these challenges, technologies like microencapsulation protect amino acids from environmental factors, enhancing their stability. Other approaches, like pairing amino acids with minerals in chelated forms, not only improve stability but also enhance the absorption of both components. These innovations allow amino acids to be used effectively across diverse applications, from functional foods to advanced supplements.

## Slide 30

Source: speaker notes

Plant-based amino acid blends are increasingly popular as sustainable and ethical alternatives to animal-based proteins. The challenge with plant proteins, like pea or rice protein, is that they often lack one or more essential amino acids, making them incomplete proteins. To overcome this, blends of complementary plant proteins are used to mimic the amino acid profiles of animal proteins, ensuring a balanced intake.

For example, pea protein is high in lysine but low in methionine, while rice protein is the opposite. Combining these two creates a complete amino acid profile, comparable to whey or egg protein. These blends are commonly found in vegan protein powders, snacks, and meal replacements.

Beyond basic nutrition, plant-based amino acids are used to develop analogues of functional proteins like collagen. By isolating and combining specific amino acids, companies can replicate the benefits of animal-derived products while catering to plant-based consumers. This innovation is driving growth in plant-based markets, particularly in sports nutrition and beauty.

## Slide 31

Source: speaker notes

While plant-based amino acid blends can replicate the amino acid profile of animal-derived proteins like hydrolyzed collagen, they face significant limitations.

Hydrolyzed collagen is composed of peptides—short chains of amino acids—that have unique structural and functional properties. These peptides are responsible for collagen’s benefits, such as promoting skin elasticity, hydration, and joint health.

A blend of single amino acids, even with the same profile as collagen, does not have the same peptide structure. This lack of structure means it cannot mimic the bioactivity of true collagen peptides, such as their ability to stimulate collagen production in the skin or provide structural support in connective tissues.

Additionally, plant-based sources often require significant processing to isolate and combine amino acids, which can impact cost, sustainability, and scalability. While these blends are a step forward in offering alternatives to animal-derived collagen, they are not yet able to fully replace the functional benefits of true collagen peptides.

## Slide 32

Source: speaker notes

The science of amino acids continues to evolve, leading to new innovations in stability, delivery, and functionality. One key area of development is in targeted delivery systems, such as liposomal or enteric-coated amino acids. These technologies protect amino acids as they pass through the digestive system, ensuring they reach the intended site of absorption, like the intestines, for maximum effectiveness.

Another innovation is in fermentation-derived amino acids, which provide a sustainable and scalable alternative to traditional extraction methods. This process is particularly important for amino acids like tryptophan and lysine, which are challenging to source naturally at scale.

We’re also seeing advancements in amino acid blends for functional applications, such as analogues for specialty proteins. For instance, companies are working on plant-based alternatives to whey protein and collagen that incorporate optimized amino acid profiles for improved functionality and taste.

Lastly, research is focusing on the synergistic effects of amino acids with other nutrients. For example, amino acids are being combined with probiotics or minerals to enhance both stability and bioavailability in gut health and sports nutrition products. These innovations are opening up exciting possibilities for amino acids in personalized health and wellness.
