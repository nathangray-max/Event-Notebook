## Summary

This module is the commercial team's grounding in dietary fibre and prebiotics. It starts with demand: most adults fall well
short of recommended fibre intake, and a cultural wave (fibermaxxing, GLP-1) and a surge of new products sit on top of that
need. It then separates two words customers often blur. Fibre is a compositional category with legal definitions and a number
on the label. Prebiotic is a function that must be shown, and in the EU and UK the word itself is treated as an implied health
claim. The deck keeps soluble versus insoluble as a first sort, then adds three dials (viscosity, fermentability and chain
length) that predict what a fibre does, including how much gas it causes. Each ingredient is graded Gold, Silver or Bronze on
its human evidence, kept apart from whether it is a recognised prebiotic and whether a claim can go on pack. Formulation dials
(dose, taste, ease of use, stability) decide whether an ingredient fits a format at all. The module ends with the authorised
claims list, a quick-pick by customer brief, a five-link decision chain and our H2 2026 priorities: the precision-prebiotic end,
the GLP-1 companion, credibility, and HMO supply.

## Key learnings

- The fibre gap is the demand driver: about 5% of men and 9% of women reach recommended daily fibre, a shortfall of about
  14 g a day (16 g actual against 25–38 g), on US data (slide 2).
- "Fibre" is a regulated number you can almost always put on a panel; "prebiotic" is a claim you must earn, and in the EU, UK
  and Canada it is an implied health claim (slide 26).
- Solubility alone predicts little. Viscosity predicts cholesterol and glucose effects, fermentability predicts SCFAs and gas,
  and chain length predicts the speed and site of fermentation, so tolerability (slide 11).
- Keep three questions apart: is it a recognised prebiotic, how strong is the human evidence for this benefit (Gold, Silver,
  Bronze), and can we say it on pack here (slide 17). Inulin, FOS and GOS are the deck's "only three you can call prebiotic
  with full confidence", and GOS works at much lower doses (slide 19).
- Gentle is a selling point: inulin and FOS are gas-prone with a tolerance ceiling of about 5–10 g a day in one serving, while
  PHGG, acacia and resistant maltodextrin are fermented slowly and suit the sensitive-gut segment (slide 12).
- Every decision runs through one chain: claim, evidence, dose, format, jurisdiction. If any link breaks, the claim breaks
  (slide 36).

## In practice

- Keep the quick-pick open in a customer meeting: GOS, XOS or RS2 for a low-dose capsule, gummy or small drink; soluble corn
  fibre, resistant wheat dextrin, resistant maltodextrin or GOS for a clear drink; PHGG, acacia or low-dose XOS for sensitive gut
  and low-FODMAP; oat β-glucan or psyllium for a cardiometabolic claim (slide 35).
- When the brief is cholesterol or glucose, go to the viscous fibres with authorised claims; the deck says not to reach for
  inulin, FOS, acacia or wheat dextrin there, as they lack the mechanism (slide 22).
- When a claim must survive a regulator or a retailer's legal team, anchor it in the authorised claims list and quote the dose
  (slides 27, 32).
- Pick the ingredient that is good enough on every dial, not the best on one: the deck's worked example chose PHGG with GOS
  because both were low-dose, clinically validated, near-tasteless and easy to use (slide 24).
- For GLP-1 users, fibre has two roles: managing GI side effects and the "fibre-first" natural GLP-1 secretion story
  (slide 29). The deck's gentle picks for a GLP-1 companion are PHGG, acacia gum and resistant dextrins (slide 35).
- Use market sizes to size an opportunity, not to forecast; the deck says estimates vary two to three times (slide 28).

## Check your understanding

- Q: Why can we almost always say "X g of fibre" on a pack but not "prebiotic" in the EU?
  A: Fibre is a regulated, quantifiable label term defined by FDA, Codex and the EU; "prebiotic" is not a regulated nutrient claim, and in the EU, UK and Canada it is an implied health claim (slide 26)
- Q: A customer wants a cholesterol claim. Should we offer inulin or oat β-glucan, and why?
  A: Oat β-glucan, which has FDA and EFSA claims at 3 g a day or more; inulin is non-viscous and lacks the mechanism (slide 22)
- Q: Which three questions does the grading method keep separate?
  A: Prebiotic status, benefit-evidence depth (Gold, Silver, Bronze) and claimability on pack (slide 17)
- Q: What is the practical tolerance ceiling for rapidly fermented short-chain prebiotics, and name two ways round it?
  A: About 5–10 g a day in a single serving; blend short and long chain, use slow or well-tolerated fibres, use low-dose-effective ingredients or escalate the dose gradually (slide 25)
- Q: What are the five links of the decision chain?
  A: Claim, evidence, dose, format and jurisdiction; if any link breaks, the claim breaks (slide 36)

## Detail

**Definitions.** Codex (2009) counts fibre from DP 10 and leaves DP 3–9 to national authorities; the US FDA (2016) and EU/UK
(Regulation 1169/2011) count from DP 3, and the FDA requires isolated or synthetic fibres to show a physiological benefit. The
deck's key point: whether microbes selectively use a fibre is irrelevant to fibre status (slide 4). For prebiotics the
scientific reference is ISAPP 2017 ("a substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health
benefit"); the deck adopts the GPA 2024 definition as our operating one, which drops the selectivity test and adds performance
benefits, so ingredients such as polyphenols can be credited with a "prebiotic effect" (slide 5). The biotic family is set out
by contrast: probiotic (Hill 2014), prebiotic (ISAPP 2017), synbiotic (ISAPP 2020) and postbiotic (ISAPP 2021) (slide 6). In the
Venn diagram, lactulose, HMOs, polyphenols and omega-3 sit outside fibre: lactulose is too small (DP 2), HMO fibre status is
contested, and polyphenols and omega-3 are not carbohydrates (slide 7).

**Mechanism.** Fibre escapes digestion in the small intestine and is fermented in the colon into short-chain fatty acids
(slide 9). Butyrate fuels colon cells and supports the gut barrier; propionate goes to the liver; acetate is the most abundant
and reaches the circulation. The deck links SCFAs to satiety hormones (GLP-1, PYY via FFAR2/FFAR3), immunity and the gut-brain
axis, and notes that some of these effects are better established than others (slide 10).

**Taxonomy and hard cases.** Two tables classify the common fibres by solubility, viscosity, fermentability and prebiotic status
(slides 14-15). The hard cases: methylcellulose is soluble and viscous but not fermented, so not a prebiotic; glucomannan is the
most viscous common fibre; acacia is soluble but non-viscous and very well tolerated; lignin is not a carbohydrate. IMO is "the
trap": much of it is digested and an FDA petition was denied. Resistant starch has five subtypes (RS1-RS5), and the deck says to
specify the type when claiming (slides 15-16).

**Evidence tiers, as the deck gives them.** Gold: psyllium (Jovanovski 2018, 28 RCTs, about 10 g a day lowered LDL by 0.33
mmol/L; dose 7–10 g a day with at least 250 mL water) and oat or barley β-glucan (Ho 2016, 58 RCTs, 3.5 g a day: LDL −4.2%;
competitor brands OatWell, PromOat) (slide 18); inulin (5–20 g a day; Orafti, Fibruline), FOS (5–21 g a day; Parnell and Reimer
2009, 21 g a day, −1.03 kg over 12 weeks; Orafti P95, Frutalose) and GOS (1.4–7 g a day; Bimuno, Vivinal/Biotis, Oligomate)
(slide 19). Silver: resistant starch (200+ human trials but heterogeneous; Hi-Maize/VersaFibe, Fibersym RW4, Solnul) and PHGG
(RCT n=108 on bloating; 5–7 g a day; Sunfiber) (slide 20). Bronze: XOS (Finegold 2014, 1.4–2.8 g a day; PreticX, Longlive),
AXOS (about 10 g a day; NAXUS, Arrabina), larch arabinogalactan (Riede 2013, 4.5 g a day; ResistAid) and native guar, which
the deck says PHGG has largely superseded (slide 21). Bronze means the breadth of human evidence is thin, not that it does not
work. The master tables (slides 30-31) add resistant dextrin, resistant maltodextrin, polydextrose, acacia, pectin, glucomannan,
lactulose, wheat dextrin, HMOs and branded botanicals (Solnul, AlphaGOS, Actazin/Livaux, Eldercraft, 360GUT, Baobest), with the
note to annotate each one's real benefit.

**Formulation.** Effective dose, taste, ease of use and stability decide fit: a 10 g fibre will not fit a capsule, gummy or
small drink (slide 23). Gas is "the silent compliance-killer", and a product nobody can tolerate is not bought again (slide 25).

**Claims.** The authorised list (slide 32): oat or barley β-glucan for cholesterol and CHD risk (FDA 1997, EFSA 2010; at least
3 g a day) and post-prandial glycaemia (EFSA; 4 g per 30 g carbohydrate); psyllium for CHD risk (FDA 1998; at least 7 g a day
soluble fibre); pectin for normal blood cholesterol (EFSA 2010; at least 6 g a day) and post-prandial glycaemia (at least 10 g
per meal); glucomannan for weight loss in a calorie-restricted diet (EFSA 2010; 3 g a day as 1 g three times); chicory inulin
for normal defecation and stool frequency (EU Reg. 2015/2314; at least 12 g a day); high-amylose maize RS2 "may reduce risk of
type 2 diabetes" (FDA 2016, qualified). Only the EFSA and EU entries apply to European packs.

**Market.** Prebiotics $6–11B and dietary fibre $9–14B (Grand View; FMI). Estimates vary two to three times depending on
whether ingredient or finished product and animal feed are counted; inulin is the largest single prebiotic (about 30–46% share),
gut health the largest segment, and Europe (about 40%) and APAC vie for the lead (slide 28). On GLP-1: about 30M+ US adults have
taken a GLP-1 (about 18%; 12% currently), digestive supplements among GLP-1 users +52% and weight-management formulas −3%
year on year; the slide names no source (slide 29).

**Reference tables.** Slides 33-34 reproduce the GPA table of established, novel and emerging prebiotics with doses and study
outcomes from Deehan et al., Advances in Nutrition 2024;15:100329, Table 1 (open access, CC BY 4.0; attribution required).

**Our H2 2026 priorities.** Lead at the precision-prebiotic end (low-dose, validated, palatable, blendable: PHGG, GOS, scFOS,
XOS and novel polyphenols, while commodity inulin and FOS keep the volume); build the GLP-1 companion; make credibility the moat
(honest dosing, evidence-matched claims, branded clinical ingredients); secure precision-fermentation supply of HMOs ahead of
adult-nutrition expansion (slide 37).
