# Prebiotics & Fibre: The Comprehensive Commercial Curriculum. Narration

## Slide 1

Source: draft

This is our commercial curriculum on prebiotics and fibre, written for the commercial team in the second half of 2026. Its aim is category fluency. It runs through definitions, mechanism, evidence, formulation, claims, the market and the ingredients themselves, and it ends with a decision framework and our priorities for the half.

## Slide 2

Source: draft

Everything starts with the fibre gap. In the US data on this slide, about 5% of men and 9% of women reach the recommended daily fibre, a shortfall of about 14 grams: 16 grams eaten against 25 to 38 recommended. The deck cites a WHO-commissioned Lancet review from 2019, Reynolds and colleagues, which pooled 185 observational studies and 58 trials and reported lower death and disease rates, by 5 to 27%, with every extra 8 grams a day.

## Slide 3

Source: draft

The deck sums up demand as a perfect storm of three forces. First, a real need: the fibre gap, which is structural and backed by the Lancet review. Second, a cultural wave: fibremaxxing, GLP-1 drugs, and the idea that "fibre is the new protein". Third, a product explosion: sodas, snacks and blends, and billion-dollar exits. Together, the deck says, they make durable demand and a growth opportunity.

## Slide 4

Source: draft

What counts as fibre? The test is that it is not digested in the small intestine and, for isolated or added fibres, shows a physiological benefit. The definitions differ on chain length. Codex sets a degree of polymerisation of 10 or more and leaves 3 to 9 to national authorities. The US FDA and the EU and UK both use 3 or more. Note the key point: whether microbes selectively use it does not matter for fibre status.

## Slide 5

Source: draft

What is a prebiotic? The scientific reference is ISAPP's 2017 definition: a substrate selectively used by host microorganisms, giving a health benefit. The one we operate on is the Global Prebiotic Association's 2024 definition: a compound used by the microbiota, producing a health or performance benefit. It drops the strict selectivity test and adds performance. That lets us credit ingredients such as polyphenols with a prebiotic effect without forcing the label "prebiotic" on them.

## Slide 6

Source: draft

The biotic family is easiest to learn by contrast. Probiotics are live microorganisms that, in adequate amounts, confer a benefit: "the bugs". Prebiotics are the substrate those microbes use: "food for the host's bugs". Synbiotics combine the two. Postbiotics are inanimate microbes and their parts: "the spent bugs". Note that purified metabolites alone do not qualify as postbiotics.

## Slide 7

Source: draft

This Venn diagram is the centrepiece. Some things are fibre only: cellulose, wheat bran, lignin, psyllium. Some are both: inulin, FOS and GOS. And some are prebiotic but not classic fibre. Lactulose is too small to count as fibre. HMOs feed infant bifidobacteria, but their fibre status is contested. Polyphenols and omega-3 are not carbohydrates at all, which is why the prebiotic circle reaches outside the fibre umbrella.

## Slide 8

Source: draft

Start with the model customers already know: soluble and insoluble. Soluble fibre dissolves in water, often forming a gel, and slows digestion; the slide's list also mentions blood sugar and cholesterol, which later slides tie to specific viscous fibres at set doses. Insoluble fibre does not dissolve; it adds bulk and speeds transit. The standard advice is to eat a mix. It is a real, lab-measured distinction and a useful first cut, and the course builds on it.

## Slide 9

Source: draft

Follow fibre through the gut. In the mouth and small intestine our enzymes break down starch and sugars, which are absorbed there. Fibre's bonds resist those enzymes, so it passes through intact. In the colon, trillions of microbes carry the enzymes we lack and ferment what arrives, producing short-chain fatty acids. So the colon, not the stomach, is the reaction vessel.

## Slide 10

Source: draft

The deck says one chain explains most of the benefits we sell. Microbes make three short-chain fatty acids. Butyrate is the preferred fuel for the cells lining the colon. Propionate goes to the liver. Acetate, the most abundant, enters the circulation. From there the deck traces links to satiety hormones, since SCFAs can stimulate GLP-1 and PYY, to immunity and to the gut-brain axis. As the slide itself says, some of these effects are better established than others.

## Slide 11

Source: draft

Four dials predict what a fibre will do. Solubility is the familiar starting point but a weak predictor on its own. Viscosity, whether it forms a gel, predicts cholesterol and glucose effects, as with beta-glucan, psyllium and guar. Fermentability predicts SCFAs and gas, as with inulin and FOS. Chain length predicts how fast and where it ferments, and so how well it is tolerated. When a customer asks what a fibre will do, reason through these dials.

## Slide 12

Source: draft

Tolerability is a design lever. Fast, short-chain fibres such as inulin and FOS are powerful but gas-prone, with a ceiling of about 5 to 10 grams a day in a single serving. Slow or partly fermented fibres, such as PHGG, acacia and resistant maltodextrin or soluble corn fibre, are gentler, which opens the sensitive-gut, IBS-adjacent and low-FODMAP segment. Match ferment speed to the customer. Gentle is a feature: sell it.

## Slide 13

Source: draft

There are four ways to classify fibre, and each predicts something different. Soluble versus insoluble is traditional and measurable, a useful first guide. Viscous versus non-viscous is linked to satiety and to cholesterol and glucose benefits. Fermentable or not is linked to microbiome benefits and to gas. Origin, whether intact, isolated or synthetic, matters for clean-label and sourcing questions, such as vegan or organic. Use viscosity and fermentability as the working lenses.

## Slide 14

Source: draft

This is the first half of the fibre taxonomy: the structural, viscous and gel-forming fibres. For each one the table gives what it is, then whether it is soluble, viscous, fermentable and prebiotic. Cellulose, lignin and wheat bran are insoluble and not prebiotic. Beta-glucan and pectin are soluble gels. Psyllium forms a gel but resists fermentation. Glucomannan has the highest viscosity. Keep this table to hand rather than memorising it.

## Slide 15

Source: draft

The second half covers the fermentable and oligosaccharide fibres, the prebiotic end. Inulin, FOS and GOS are soluble, rapidly fermented and established prebiotics. Resistant starch, acacia and PHGG are also listed as established. Resistant maltodextrin and polydextrose are slower to ferment and graded novel. The same columns apply: soluble, viscous, fermentable, prebiotic, so you can compare any two fibres at a glance.

## Slide 16

Source: draft

These are the cases that trip people up. Methylcellulose is soluble and viscous but not fermented, so it is not a prebiotic. Glucomannan is the most viscous common fibre, with minimal prebiotic effect. Acacia is soluble but not viscous, slowly fermented and very well tolerated. Lignin is not a carbohydrate at all. And resistant starch has five subtypes, RS1 to RS5, so always specify the type when claiming.

## Slide 17

Source: draft

The deck grades evidence Gold, Silver or Bronze. Gold means a large body of good human trials or meta-analyses and, or, authorised health claims. Silver means moderate, growing human evidence. Bronze means limited or emerging evidence. Keep three questions apart: is it a recognised prebiotic, how strong is the human evidence for this particular benefit, and can we use the word on pack here? Clinical evidence is not the same as commercial maturity.

## Slide 18

Source: draft

The first Gold tier is psyllium and oat or barley beta-glucan, the regulator-backed viscous fibres. Psyllium: a review of 28 trials found about 10 grams a day lowered LDL by 0.33 millimoles per litre; the dose is 7 to 10 grams with at least 250 millilitres of water. Beta-glucan carries FDA and EFSA cholesterol claims at 3 grams a day or more. Neither is a prebiotic: the effect is physical, and the claim only holds at the dose.

## Slide 19

Source: draft

The second Gold tier is the three proven prebiotics: inulin, FOS and GOS, the only three the deck says you can call prebiotic with full confidence. All three are bifidogenic. Inulin's dose is 5 to 20 grams a day, FOS 5 to 21. GOS works at much lower doses, 1.4 to 7 grams, and the slide cites studies on IBS, traveller's diarrhoea and immunity. Competitor brands are named for each, such as Orafti, Frutalose and Bimuno.

## Slide 20

Source: draft

Silver tier: resistant starch and PHGG. Resistant starch has over 200 human trials, but they vary, so specify the type and the endpoint; the dose range here is 3.5 to 30 grams a day. PHGG, at 5 to 7 grams, is low-FODMAP. The slide cites a trial of 108 people that reported less bloating than placebo, and notes a Health Canada IBS claim. The deck calls it a quiet workhorse for sensitive-gut positioning.

## Slide 21

Source: draft

Bronze tier: low-dose and emerging fibres. Bronze means the human evidence is thin, not that the ingredient does not work. XOS raised bifidobacteria at 1.4 to 2.8 grams a day in one small study. Arabinoxylan from wheat has an EFSA glycaemia claim. For larch arabinogalactan the slide cites a trial of 199 people at 4.5 grams a day that reported fewer colds: 58.4% against 72.4%. Native guar is largely superseded by PHGG.

## Slide 22

Source: draft

Benefit by benefit, cholesterol and blood glucose belong to the viscous fibres. For LDL cholesterol the claims sit at dose thresholds: beta-glucan at 3 grams a day or more, psyllium 7, pectin 6. For post-meal glucose: beta-glucan at 4 grams per 30 grams of carbohydrate, and pectin at 10 grams per meal. Inulin, FOS, acacia and wheat dextrin are not cholesterol ingredients; they lack the mechanism. For a cardiometabolic brief, reach for the viscous fibres.

## Slide 23

Source: draft

Science is only half the choice; the other half is whether we can use it. Four formulation dials. Effective dose: how many grams does it take, since a low dose fits more formats. Taste: neutral, sweet or off-note. Ease of use: solubility, mouthfeel, grittiness and processing. Stability: does it survive heat, low pH and shelf life? A 10-gram fibre will not fit a capsule, a gummy or a small drink.

## Slide 24

Source: draft

The winner sits where behaviour and formulation overlap. The worked example on this slide is a product the deck calls Advanced Biotics, which uses PHGG with GOS, the Bimuno brand. Both were chosen because they cleared validation, dose, taste and handling at once: low-dose, clinically validated, near-tasteless and easy to use. Higher-dose or off-tasting options failed the formulation dials. Do not pick the best on one dial; pick the one good enough on all.

## Slide 25

Source: draft

Gas is the silent compliance-killer. Rapidly fermented short-chain prebiotics such as FOS and short-chain inulin ferment early in the colon and make gas, with a ceiling of about 5 to 10 grams in a single serving. The deck gives four solutions: blend short and long chains, use slow, well-tolerated fibres, choose ingredients that work at low doses, and build the dose up gradually. A product nobody can tolerate does not get bought again.

## Slide 26

Source: draft

Here is the core difference in claims. "Dietary fibre" is a regulated label term: you can put it on the nutrition panel, and you can almost always say how many grams of fibre a product has. "Prebiotic" is not a regulated nutrient claim. In the US it is a structure-function descriptor; in the EU, UK and Canada it is treated as an implied health claim. Fibre is a number; prebiotic is a claim you must earn.

## Slide 27

Source: draft

This is the deck's list of authorised claims to anchor on, each with its authority and dose. In the EU: beta-glucan for cholesterol, from 3 grams a day; glucomannan for weight loss within a calorie-restricted diet, 3 grams a day; pectin for cholesterol and glucose; and chicory inulin for normal defecation, from 12 grams a day. Psyllium's cholesterol claim and the RS2 type 2 diabetes claim are US FDA claims. Always quote the dose.

## Slide 28

Source: draft

The market is big and growing, but treat the figures as directional. Prebiotics are put at 6 to 11 billion dollars and dietary fibre at 9 to 14 billion, from Grand View and FMI, and estimates vary two to threefold by definition. Inulin is the largest single prebiotic, at about 30 to 46% share, and gut health the largest segment, with Europe, at about 40%, and Asia-Pacific vying for the lead.

## Slide 29

Source: draft

GLP-1 is the deck's highest-momentum white space. The slide gives about 30 million or more US adults who have taken a GLP-1 drug, about 18%, with 12% taking one now; digestive supplements up 52% among users, and weight-management formulas down 3% year on year. Fibre plays two roles: managing side effects such as constipation and tolerability, and what the deck calls a "fibre-first" GLP-1 secretion story, a mechanism it links to SCFAs.

## Slide 35

Source: draft

This is the quick pick: brief to shortlist. For a low-dose format, GOS, XOS or RS2. For a clear, stable drink, soluble corn fibre, resistant wheat dextrin, resistant maltodextrin or GOS. For a sensitive gut, PHGG, acacia or low-dose XOS. For a cardiometabolic claim, oat beta-glucan or psyllium. For a GLP-1 companion, PHGG, acacia or resistant dextrins. Keep it open in customer meetings.

## Slide 36

Source: draft

Every commercial decision runs through one chain. Claim: what benefit do we want to say? Evidence: is it Gold, Silver or Bronze for that benefit? Dose: does the serving carry the studied dose? Format: can the format physically carry it? Jurisdiction: is the claim lawful in that market? If any link breaks, the claim breaks, and the break shows what to fix: the ingredient, the dose, the format, or the claim.

## Slide 37

Source: draft

Our four priorities for the second half of 2026. Lead at the precision-prebiotic end: low-dose, validated, palatable ingredients such as PHGG, GOS, scFOS and XOS. Build the GLP-1 companion: gentle, nutrient-dense, tolerability-first products. Make credibility the moat: honest dosing, claims matched to evidence, and branded clinical ingredients. And secure precision-fermentation supply of HMOs, ahead of growth in adult nutrition.
