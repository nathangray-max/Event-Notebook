## Summary

This module introduces functional mushrooms: mushrooms valued for bioactive compounds rather than flavour, with a long
history in Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda and other traditions and now one of the fastest-growing sectors in
health and wellness. It covers the five the deck treats as key (reishi, lion's mane, cordyceps, chaga and turkey tail),
the benefits it associates with each, and the compounds behind them, from beta-glucans and triterpenoids to the
hericenones and erinacines of lion's mane and the cordycepin of cordyceps.

It explains the choice between fruiting body and mycelium, then where mushrooms are used: supplements, functional foods
and drinks such as mushroom coffee, nutricosmetics and sports nutrition. Retail data from SPINS shows mushrooms among
the fastest-growing functional ingredients in ready-to-drink and functional beverages.

The second half is about why our material is worth buying. Standardisation keeps bioactive levels consistent,
contamination testing covers heavy metals, mycotoxins and pesticides, and some mushrooms are grown on peanut substrates,
a hidden allergen risk. Our mushrooms are sourced on non-peanut substrates, which the deck calls a great USP. Quality,
transparency and allergen safety are its success factors.

## Key learnings

- Functional mushrooms contain bioactive compounds with specific health benefits and have been used in traditional medicine for centuries (slide 3) (slide 4).
- The key mushrooms and the benefits the deck links them to: reishi (stress, immune), lion's mane (cognitive), cordyceps (energy, endurance), chaga (antioxidant), turkey tail (gut and immune) (slide 5).
- The fruiting body is often richer in beta-glucans; mycelium offers scalability, lower cost and compounds such as ergothioneine (slide 7).
- Mushrooms appear in supplements, functional foods and beverages (mushroom coffee, tea blends, bars), nutricosmetics and sports nutrition (slide 13).
- In SPINS data, reishi grew 71% and cordyceps 52% by dollars year on year in RTD beverages, and mushroom sales in food and beverage were $642M, up 19.5% (slide 15).
- Standardisation, contamination testing and allergen-safe sourcing build trust; our mushrooms are sourced on non-peanut substrates (slide 16) (slide 17).

## In practice

- Lead with allergen safety: some mushrooms are grown on peanut or peanut-shell substrates, and our non-peanut sourcing is, in the deck's words, a great USP (slide 17).
- Talk standardisation: consistent levels of bioactives such as beta-glucans and triterpenoids, for example reishi standardised for triterpenoids (slide 16).
- Help the customer choose fruiting body or mycelium by the compounds and benefits they want and by cost and scale (slide 7).
- Point beverage customers to the growth data: kombucha and functional drinks, shelf-stable tea and coffee RTD, and lion's mane and turkey tail in functional beverages (slide 15).
- Keep to the deck's hedges with customers: lion's mane compounds "may stimulate" nerve growth factor (slide 3).

## Check your understanding

- Q: What makes a mushroom "functional" rather than culinary?
  A: It contains bioactive compounds with specific health benefits (slide 3).
- Q: Which part of the mushroom is often richer in beta-glucans, and what does mycelium offer instead?
  A: The fruiting body; mycelium is scalable, cost-effective and offers compounds such as ergothioneine (slide 7).
- Q: Which compounds are unique to lion's mane?
  A: Hericenones and erinacines (slide 3) (slide 8).
- Q: Why does substrate matter, and what is our position?
  A: Some mushrooms are grown on peanut or peanut-shell substrates, a hidden allergen risk; ours are sourced on non-peanut substrates (slide 17).
- Q: What does contamination testing check for?
  A: Heavy metals, mycotoxins and pesticides (slide 16).
