## Summary

This module is the starting point for the vitamin side of our range. It defines a vitamin as an organic compound the body
needs in small amounts and cannot make in sufficient quantities, so it must come from food, supplements or fortified
products. It splits vitamins into water-soluble ones, which are not stored and are needed regularly, and fat-soluble ones,
which are stored and can build up to toxic levels.

It then walks through what each vitamin does, explains why the alphabet has gaps (substances once called vitamins, such
as "vitamin H", were later reclassified or renamed), and introduces the forms a vitamin can take: provitamin, active,
synthetic and coenzyme. Bioavailability and conversion efficiency explain why form matters, especially for older people
or those with genetic variations such as MTHFR. Vitamins also work together, which matters when building a
multivitamin.

The last slide is the commercial point. Vitamins often need stabilising, coating or encapsulating, and the right choice
depends on the customer's delivery format, other ingredients, taste profile and budget or positioning. Knowing the
forms, and asking the right questions, is how we match an ingredient to a brief.

## Key learnings

- Vitamins are organic compounds needed in small quantities that the body cannot produce in sufficient amounts; they act as coenzymes or antioxidants (slide 2).
- Water-soluble vitamins are not stored in large amounts and are needed regularly; fat-soluble vitamins are stored in fatty tissue and can accumulate to toxic levels if taken in excess (slide 4).
- Missing letters and out-of-order B numbers come from reclassification: "vitamin H" is now biotin (B7), "vitamin F" the essential fatty acids, "vitamin M" folic acid (slide 7).
- Vitamins come as provitamins (beta-carotene to vitamin A), active forms (retinol), synthetic forms (folic acid) and coenzyme forms (methylcobalamin) (slide 8).
- Bioavailability depends on the form, digestive health and nutrient interactions; conversion efficiency on genetics (MTHFR), age and health status (slide 10).
- Vitamins work in synergy, for example vitamin D with calcium and vitamin C with non-heme iron, which matters when formulating multivitamins (slide 11).

## In practice

- Start with the customer's needs: delivery format (capsule, softgel, bulk tub, stick pack, liquid shot, carbonated drink, protein bar, brownie), other ingredients, taste profile and budget or brand positioning, basic or premium (slide 12).
- Expect to offer stabilised, coated or encapsulated forms: they change solubility, heat stability, bioavailability, oxidation stability and taste (slide 12).
- Customers still ask for old names such as "vitamin H"; the reclassification table lets you translate them (slide 7, speaker notes).
- Form is a selling point: the deck says people with certain conditions or who are ageing may benefit from active forms that bypass conversion (slide 10).
- My reading: the synergy pairs on slide 11 (D with calcium, B12 with folate, D with K2) are natural starting points for a premix conversation.

## Check your understanding

- Q: Why do fat-soluble vitamins carry a toxicity risk that water-soluble ones mostly do not?
  A: They are stored in the body's fatty tissues and can accumulate to toxic levels if taken in excess; excess water-soluble vitamins are usually excreted in urine (slide 4).
- Q: A customer asks for "vitamin H". What do they mean?
  A: Biotin, vitamin B7, recognised as part of the B-complex (slide 7).
- Q: What is the difference between folic acid and folate in the deck's terms?
  A: Folic acid is the synthetic form of folate (vitamin B9): more stable in supplements, but it must be converted to the active form in the body (slide 8).
- Q: Name three factors that affect conversion efficiency.
  A: Genetics (e.g. MTHFR variation and folic acid), age (e.g. beta-carotene to vitamin A, B12 conversion) and health status such as liver or kidney disease (slide 10).
- Q: What four things does the deck say may affect which vitamin form you recommend?
  A: Delivery format, other ingredients, taste profile, and budget or brand positioning (slide 12).
