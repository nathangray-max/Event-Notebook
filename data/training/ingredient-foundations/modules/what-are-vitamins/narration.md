## Slide 2

Source: speaker notes

Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential in small amounts for various biological processes that sustain life. Unlike macronutrients like carbohydrates, proteins, or fats, which the body needs in larger quantities, vitamins are required in minute amounts to catalyze and regulate key biochemical reactions.

Since the human body either cannot synthesize these compounds at all or cannot produce them in sufficient amounts, vitamins must be obtained from external sources such as food, supplements, or fortified products.

Most vitamins act as coenzymes, meaning they assist enzymes in their role of catalyzing chemical reactions. Some vitamins also serve as antioxidants, helping to protect cells from damage caused by free radicals.

## Slide 4

Source: speaker notes

Vitamins are vital for maintaining overall health and preventing disease. Without them, the body cannot function properly. Each vitamin has specific roles, such as promoting energy metabolism, supporting immune function, aiding in cell and tissue repair, and contributing to neurological functions. Deficiencies in certain vitamins can lead to a range of health issues, from fatigue and weakened immunity to more serious conditions like scurvy, rickets, or neurological disorders.

For instance, Vitamin D is crucial for calcium absorption, which is essential for bone health, while Vitamin C plays a major role in collagen synthesis and immune support. The right balance of vitamins is essential for optimal well-being, energy, and longevity.

## Slide 7

Source: speaker notes

You might notice that some letters are missing in the sequence of vitamin names (e.g., there's no Vitamin F, H, or I).

This is because, as our understanding of these compounds evolved, some substances originally thought to be vitamins were later reclassified.

For example, what was once called "Vitamin F" is now known to be essential fatty acids (like Omega-3 and Omega-6).

Similarly, some vitamins were grouped together under a common name, such as the B-complex vitamins, which were originally thought to be individual vitamins.

It's important to be aware of some of this because even now, some people will ask us for Vitamin H, for example.

## Slide 8

Source: draft

A vitamin can come in different forms, and the slide sets out four. Provitamins are converted by the body into the vitamin: beta-carotene becomes vitamin A. Active forms are ready to use, like retinol. Synthetic forms are made for stability or shelf life: folic acid, the synthetic form of folate, is more stable in supplements but must be converted in the body. Coenzyme forms, such as methylcobalamin for B12, need no further conversion; the deck describes it as more bioavailable than cyanocobalamin.

## Slide 10

Source: draft

Two ideas decide how well a vitamin works in the body. Bioavailability is how much is absorbed and used. It depends on the form, on digestive health, and on other nutrients: fat helps absorb vitamin D, while phytic acid in grains inhibits iron absorption. Conversion efficiency is how well the body turns a provitamin into its active form, and genetics, such as MTHFR with folic acid, age and health status all affect it. The slide's point: people with certain conditions, or who are ageing, may benefit from active forms.

## Slide 11

Source: draft

Vitamins rarely work alone, and the slide calls this synergy: one nutrient supporting another. Vitamin D increases calcium absorption in the gut. Vitamin C enhances absorption of non-heme iron from plant foods. B12 and folate are both needed for DNA synthesis and red blood cell formation, and vitamin C regenerates vitamin E's antioxidant capacity. The second table pairs vitamins with other nutrients, such as vitamin A with zinc and B6 with magnesium. The deck's point for us: these pairings matter when formulating a multivitamin.
