# Unfiltered: An Insider's View of the Whey Protein Market

## Summary
Alan Johnstone, who has spent most of the last decades selling whey protein, most recently at Arla Foods Ingredients, and now runs Nutrilution, explained why whey prices hit records this year and what buyers should do about it.

His answer starts with milk. Whey protein comes from cheese, and cheese production grows only about one and a half percent a year, while demand for whey protein grows by 10% or more: sports nutrition, GLP-1 users, protein in everyday food, and clear whey. Filtration plants are expensive and take years to build, and producers have put the whey they have into isolate, so concentrate was short when brands had just switched to it.

Prices then swing further than supply and demand alone would explain, because buyers stock up when prices rise and wait when they fall. That is happening now: concentrate is falling fast, isolate is correcting, and the proteins brands moved into are rising.

His advice was plain. Do not try to time the bottom. Keep buying steadily if today's price lets you make money. And accept that whey will stay expensive, because the supply limit is structural.

## Key takeaways
- The core constraint: cheese production grows about 1.5% a year, while whey protein demand grows over 10%. He called it the most important conclusion of the talk [00:16] (photo 7) (slide 5).
- Producers chose isolate. Over five years WPI grew 15.0% a year and WPC80 1.5%; clear WPI is now about a quarter of all isolate (photo 9) (slide 8).
- Between 25 August and 4 November 2026, WPC80 fell 40.7% to €16.00/kg and WPI 20.3% to €25.50/kg, while MPC85 rose 13.5% to €15.80/kg (photo 18) (slide 12).
- New capacity will not close the gap: he forecast WPI at 7 to 11% a year and WPC80 at 4 to 7% to 2030, and both cannot reach the top of their range [00:16] (photo 11) (slide 9).
- Mainstream buyers choose on taste and texture, not protein source, and whey is the easiest protein to make taste good [00:16].
- For buyers: "Don't try to time the market. Keep inventory steady. Whey will be expensive." (photo 20) (slide 13)

- The route from milk: cheese keeps the casein and fat; liquid whey goes through ultrafiltration and drying to WPC (35 to 80% protein), microfiltration or ion exchange to WPI (90% or more), then acidification or hydrolysis to clear or hydrolysed WPI. Sweet whey powder (about 12% protein), permeate, lactose, demineralised whey (D70, D90 for infant nutrition) and whey cream are the other outlets for the same whey [00:16] (photo 4) (slide 4).
- Three bottlenecks: factories ("CAPEX intensive, less flexible than people expect, takes years to start commercial production"), liquid whey, and by-products, "a major influencer of producer value". Making WPC80 leaves permeate and lactose, which are easy to sell; WPI makers must also sell a fatty protein stream, shown on the slide as whey cream (Procream or WFC) [00:16] (photo 6) (photo 7) (slide 5) (slide 6).

- The buying cycle, using COVID as the example: longer lead times, more stock, rising demand, buyers competing, rising prices and an incentive to buy early; then the reverse, with the 2022 peak as logistics freed up and buyers destocked, and the 2023 trough as buyers waited with stocks too low [00:16] (photo 13) (photo 14) (photo 15) (slide 10) (slide 11).
- Now: new US plants planned three or four years ago are commissioning; isolate, WPC35 and sweet whey powder are better uses of whey, so WPC80 output should fall; substitutes (MPC85, MPI, caseins, plant proteins) are rising, and WPC80 and milk protein "should never meet" [00:16].

- Forces for the next move. WPC80, up: strong other derivatives, rising alternatives, long-term protein demand; down: high inventories, powerful momentum, demand destruction. WPI, up: smaller share of brand costs, healthy inventories on both sides, harder to replace; down: demand destruction, production increases due (photo 16) (slide 12).
