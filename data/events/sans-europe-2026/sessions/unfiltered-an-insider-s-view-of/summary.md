# Unfiltered: An Insider's View of the Whey Protein Market

## Summary
Alan Johnstone (owner of Nutrilution, formerly selling whey at Arla Foods Ingredients) explained the supply chain behind this year's whey prices [00:16]. On Vesper's EU index WPI rose from a trough of about EUR 7 a kilogram in 2023 to EUR 32.00 on 25 August 2026, and WPC80, up roughly sixfold since 2020, jumped from about 12 euros to 28 in about six weeks, which hurt more because brands had already switched into WPC80 [00:16] (photo 2) (photo 16). Demand is growing at over 10% a year (sports nutrition, GLP-1 users, mainstream protein, clear whey, which is almost all WPI), while liquid whey only grows with cheese production at about 1.5% a year, and filtration plants are expensive and slow to build [00:16] (photo 7). Over five years producers grew WPI at 15.0% a year (80,494 t in 2020 to 161,800 t in 2025) and WPC80 at 1.5% (334,000 t to 360,000 t); new capacity suggests WPI at 7 to 11% and WPC80 at 4 to 7% a year to 2030, but not both at the top [00:16] (photo 8) (photo 11). Prices overshoot in both directions on buyer behaviour, and between 25 August and 4 November 2026 WPC80 fell 40.7% to EUR 16.00 a kilogram and WPI 20.3% to EUR 25.50, while MPC85 rose 13.5% to EUR 15.80 and met WPC80 [00:16] (photo 18). His advice: do not try to time the bottom, keep buying steadily if the price lets you make money, and accept that whey "will be expensive. It should be expensive" [00:16] (photo 20).

## Key takeaways
- WPI went from around 7 euros a kilogram in 2023 to over 30 euros earlier this year; WPC80 held around 12 euros, then rocketed to 28 in about six weeks. WPC80 was the existential risk for brands because they had already moved their sourcing into it [00:16]. The slide puts WPI near EUR 32 a kilogram at the summer record and WPC80 up roughly sixfold since 2020, both "now correcting"; its chart (Vesper, EU monthly, December 2019 to October 2026) shows WPC80 at about EUR 5 in 2020 and 2023, peaking about EUR 12 in 2022 and about EUR 26 to 27 in mid 2026, and WPI at about EUR 8 in 2020, EUR 18 in 2022, EUR 7 in 2023 and about EUR 31 in mid 2026 (photo 2).
- Demand drivers: traditional sports nutrition at 10% a year on Euromonitor, which he thinks is a big underestimate; GLP-1 users as incremental demand; protein going mainstream (Starbucks, Papa John's, Dunkin'); clear whey, which barely existed five years ago, is one of the fastest-selling parts of the category and is almost all whey [00:16]. The slide's figures: the 2024 global sports nutrition market splits 48% protein powders, 26% protein bars, 17% non-protein and 9% protein RTD, growing 8 to 10% a year; clear whey SKUs are up 35% year on year; 12% of US adults take a GLP-1 medication, with protein spend up 20% (KFF 2025, BCG 2026); Starbucks' protein drinks carry 15 to 36 g (photo 3).
- Whey wins on taste and texture, not nutrition: "the more mainstream you get with protein products, the less people care about the protein source." It is far easier to make a good-tasting product with whey than with most alternatives [00:16].
- Supply: only 0.6% of liquid milk is whey protein (the rest by weight: water 87.3%, lactose 4.7%, fat 3.9%, casein 2.8%, minerals 0.7%); whey comes from cheese, and cheese production grows about 1.5% a year, so liquid whey does too, against demand growing over 10%. This is his most important conclusion [00:16] (photo 4) (photo 7).
- The route from milk: cheese keeps the casein and fat; liquid whey goes through ultrafiltration and drying to WPC (35 to 80% protein), microfiltration or ion exchange to WPI (90% or more), then acidification or hydrolysis to clear or hydrolysed WPI; each step is a plant, and sweet whey powder (about 12% protein), permeate, lactose, demineralised whey (D70, D90, for infant nutrition) and whey cream are the other outlets for the same whey [00:16] (photo 4).
- Factories are the first bottleneck ("phenomenally expensive" and slow to build; the slide: "CAPEX intensive, less flexible than people expect, takes years to start commercial production"); liquid whey is the second; byproducts are the third, "a major influencer of producer value", and WPI producers must also sell a fatty protein stream [00:16] (photo 6) (photo 7).
- Over five years total whey protein supply grew just under 5% a year: global WPC80 plus WPI production rose 26%, from 414,494 t in 2020 to 521,800 t in 2025 (a 4.7% CAGR); WPI grew 15.0% a year (80,494 t to 161,800 t) at the expense of WPC80 at 1.5% (334,000 t to 360,000 t), which explains why WPC80 was undersupplied this year. Clear whey may be up to 25% of WPI (top of the estimate range; 40,450 t of the 161,800 t in 2025); ordinary instant WPI grew 8.6% a year, below the category [00:16] (photo 8) (photo 9).
- New capacity: WPI expected to grow 7 to 11% a year, WPC80 4 to 7% (CAGR 2025 to 2030), but if WPI is at 11, WPC80 has to be more like 4 because there is not enough whey. Both are below underlying demand [00:16] (photo 11).
- The capacity list: announced US additions (McCully Consulting, approximate) of 24,400 t of WPC80 and 7,500 t of WPI in 2026, 41,600 t and 20,300 t in 2027, 45,900 t and 22,900 t in 2028; in the UK, EU and Oceania, FrieslandCampina (WPI expansion), Fonterra (various speciality) and Polmlek (WPC and WPI) in 2026, Tirlan (WPC, WPI and clear) and Liprovit (WPI) in 2027, and Arla's mozzarella plant in 2028. He said the European list is not exhaustive, with some Eastern European producers he could not get information from [00:16] (photo 11).
- Prices overshoot on behaviour, in both directions (the COVID example): buyers stock up when prices rise and sit out when they fall. The slide's cycle: longer lead times, inventory, demand rises, buyers compete for available material, prices rise, incentive to buy early, demand rises again, with the brand and consumer response feeding back into demand; the 2022 peak was logistics freeing up and buyers destocking, the 2023 trough buyers waiting as long as possible with stocks too low [00:16] (photo 13) (photo 15).
- Now: WPC80 "has been falling pretty hard", WPI "still expensive, but definitely correcting"; new US factories set in motion three or four years ago are commissioning; producers have better uses for whey (WPI, WPC35, sweet whey powder), so WPC80 production should fall; MPC85 and other substitutes are rising and WPC80 and milk protein lines "should never meet" [00:16]. The weekly Vesper figures: between 25 August and 4 November 2026 WPC80 fell 40.7%, from EUR 27.00 to EUR 16.00 a kilogram, WPI fell 20.3%, from EUR 32.00 to EUR 25.50, and MPC85 rose 13.5%, from EUR 13.92 to EUR 15.80, meeting WPC80 at about EUR 16 on 4 November (photo 16) (photo 18).
- The MPC85 line: brands substituted MPC85 (or MPI, caseins, plant proteins) into formulations, so those prices are rising while WPC80 falls; he quoted Keynes on markets staying irrational longer than you can stay solvent, so the lines touching does not mean they bounce apart at once, "but it can't go on forever" [00:16] (photo 18).
- What decides the next move, from the slide: for WPC80 the bullish forces are strong other derivatives, rising alternatives and long-term protein demand, the bearish ones high inventories, powerful momentum and demand destruction; for WPI, bullish are its smaller share of brand costs, healthy inventories on both sides and being harder to replace, bearish are demand destruction and production increases due (photo 16).
- Where prices go: "structurally rising but volatile"; the correction is under way and "may overshoot below trend... then demand pulls it back up". The schematic averages EU WPC80 and WPI (Vesper, monthly to October 2026) and the slide says the trend and forward path are "directional, not a forecast" [00:16] (photo 19).
- Advice: do not try to be too clever with volume; buy steadily if the price works; whey will be and should be expensive; a business that cannot cope with this year's prices needs to change its model [00:16]. The three closing takeaways on the slide: "Don't try to time the market. Keep inventory steady. Whey will be expensive." (photo 20)
- Nutrilution also sells niche, higher-value ingredients and advises buyers on whey and dairy protein sourcing, including cross-continental supply chains [00:16].

## For Vita Actives
The structural case for non-dairy protein is made by a whey insider: cheese grows 1.5% a year and demand over 10%, and he expects whey to stay expensive [00:16] (photo 7) (photo 20). For IsoClr: Pea, my reading is that this is the opening, but he also set the bar: mainstream consumers care about taste and texture, not source, and whey is the easiest protein to make taste good, so a clear pea isolate has to win on taste and clarity before it wins on supply. Clear whey is almost all WPI, is a quarter of all isolate (40,450 t in 2025) and is where the squeeze is tightest; a clear pea isolate is positioned exactly there (photo 9) (my reading). The next few months are a window: WPC80 fell 40.7% in ten weeks, MPC85 rose 13.5% to meet it, and brands have just reformulated, so they are reviewing formulations and open to blends (photo 18); my reading is that this is the moment to be in front of formulators with pea, before WPC80 settles. Nick Morgan's observation that brands want higher-margin non-protein lines applies here too: CreaWin and Oxystorm are the lines brands want to grow while whey margins are thin (my reading). Nutrilution is a potential partner or competitor in niche ingredients and sourcing advice. Relevance high: protein market structure and pricing.
