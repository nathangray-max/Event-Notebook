# From hype to proof: the science behind GLP-1 targeted nutritional supplements

## Summary
Gulrez Zariwala, founder director of the Centre for Nutraceuticals, reviewed what science supports supplements aimed at GLP-1 and satiety [00:15]. He explained that gut hormones (GLP-1 and PYY) signal fullness and ghrelin hunger, and that GLP-1 drugs give weight loss of "between five to 15%" but can cost muscle as well as fat [00:15]. Of the supplement candidates, he said proteins have "an undisputed effect" on GLP-1 secretion, while berberine, cinnamon and bitter compounds have modest effects and few human studies [00:15]. His centre's own trial found potato protein and whey protein isolate raised GLP-1 equally, and a new trial of a mulberry extract (Otter: "Redicools") found significant rises in GLP-1 and falls in appetite and "food noise" [00:15]. He closed by saying no natural supplement can match a drug's effect, so expectations need managing and products need scientific validation [00:15].

## Key takeaways
- The Centre for Nutraceuticals, in central London, aims to bridge industry and academia with independent research, from formulation and cell studies to human trials, sponsored or blue-sky [00:15].
- Obesity: incidence is high at 40% in the US and 35 to 40% in parts of South America and Africa; BMI over 30 is the simple consensus definition [00:15].
- Drug history: most obesity drugs were withdrawn; centrally acting ones such as rimonabant ("rimonaban" in Otter) carried neuropsychiatric risks, which turned the field to gut hormones [00:15].
- Gut hormones: ghrelin signals hunger; GLP-1, PYY, oxyntomodulin and GIP signal fullness; he said PYY is the more potent anorexigenic hormone, but GLP-1 drugs came first for commercial and clinical reasons [00:15].
- GLP-1 drugs give weight loss of "between five to 15%"; side effects include nausea and vomiting, loss of facial fat pads, collagen breakdown, and loss of body weight rather than body fat, so muscle loss "can lead to sarcopenia" [00:15].
- GLP-1 is not banned in sport but is "on the watch list", and he said athletes have noticed reduced inflammation and more energy [00:15].
- The supplement boom (Expo West, last year) brought "some high-profile lawsuits", regulatory clampdowns and lower consumer confidence [00:15].
- Proteins: an "undisputed effect" on GLP-1 secretion, depending on quality and amino acid profile; a 2015 study of high protein against high carbohydrate and high fat showed a significant post-meal rise in GLP-1 and PYY [00:15].
- Other candidates: OEA acts directly (GPR119) but degrades fast, so formulation may help; berberine's GLP-1 effects are indirect, mostly in rodents, and modest; cinnamon and bitter compounds have mechanisms but "mild to modest" effects and few human studies [00:15].
- His protein appetite trial (placebo-controlled triple crossover, isocaloric breakfasts): potato protein and whey protein isolate both raised GLP-1 significantly and equally; whey protein isolate also raised GIP significantly at 60 minutes, which he called the first nutraceutical showing a similar trend to the dual drugs, "not to the same magnitude" [00:15].
- The mulberry extract trial (with a UK company Otter heard as "Finova"; triple crossover with placebo, the extract, and a leading US GLP-1 brand's combination): a significant rise in GLP-1 at 180 minutes, a significant fall in appetite, and a significant fall in food noise over 24 hours ("19 with one and 21 with the other"); it finished last month, will be submitted next week, and follow-on studies are planned for 2027-28 if the partner supports them [00:15].
- Conclusions: supplements "may fill a gap" for current or lapsed users, but "There is no natural supplement solution that can match the magnitude of response" of a drug; validated products "increase market resilience"; GLP-1 use creates secondary demand for probiotics, proteins, fibre and multivitamins [00:15].
