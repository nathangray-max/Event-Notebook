Why are we extrapolating
from male-based research?
• Default body: the “70 kg male” became the reference participant.
• Hormones treated as noise: the cycle was seen as a confounder to avoid rather than a
variable to study.
• Cost and complexity: testing women properly means verifying phase with hormone
measures and testing across several phases.
• Contraception variety: it makes it harder to recruit uniform groups.
• Commercial incentives: product claims were built on the existing (male) evidence, so
there was little pressure to retest in women. This is where industry can now lead.

Quick physiology lesson
Hormone levels
Follicular first. Luteal later.
The textbook 28 -day cycle is an average,
not a rule. Cycle length varies between
people, and within the same person, month
to month.
The follicular phase is more variable (if
cycle is shorter or longer, this is usually
why).
The luteal phase is typically more
consistent and lasts ~12- 15 days after
ovulation.
The timing of the Luteinising Hormone (LH)
surge can also shift from month to month
or vary widely from woman to woman (not
exactly day 14).
Stress, sleep, body weight, training load,
and life context all affect cycle patterns.

--- page 20 ---
What about protein?
• Oestrogen is an anabolic hormone f avouring muscle
building, Progesterone is a catabolic hormone favouring
muscle breakdown.
•  In theory, an athletic female might need more protein in
luteal phase BUT with an increase in resting metabolic rate
and energy intake/hunger, they are already likely to be
increasing their protein intake.
• Female athletes should aim for high quality protein as close to
beginning and/or after completion of exercise as possible at a
dose of 0.3-0.4g/kg body weight.
• During the luteal phase aim for the upper end of the range
due to the catabolic actions of progesterone
International society of sport nutrition position stand: nutritional concerns of the female athlete (Sims et al, 2023)

--- page 23 ---
Justify
Instead of adding trending
ingredients for “oestrogen
balance”, with no evidence in
active women...
Building better products
Include only ingredients
with evidence for the
outcome you’re claiming,
e.g. creatine 3–5 g/day
alongside strength training
for midlife muscle and
strength.
Dose & Disclose
Instead of proprietary blends
with headline ingredients at
token doses, untested for
banned substances
Use the doses from the
trials, list every dose on the
label, and batch-test if you
are targeting athletes (e.g.
Informed Sport) so tested
athletes can use it safely.
Claim honestly
Instead of: “balances your
hormones”, “cycle-synced” or
other claims with no trials
behind them
Use authorised claims you
can defend, and say what
isn’t yet known. Honesty
about evidence gaps builds
credibility with women and
practitioners.
Co-design
Instead of: a pink tub on the
same formula, designed
around a male evidence base
Involve women in your
target audience, sports
nutritionists or dietitians
and researchers from day
one, and fund female-
specific studies to fill the
gaps.

--- page 24 ---
What  can the supplement industry do?
Fund female-specific trials, rather than just adding women to male study designs.
Research for your product not just the ingredient.
Allow clear publication of null results: 'no difference in women' is valuable evidence even if it wasn’t
what you aimed to find.
Ensure in that research menstrual status is verified with best-practice methods, not day-counting.
Educate around the products, ensure the target user is clear and who it will benefit.

--- page 25 ---
Take home messages • More high quality research is needed in the active female population across
the lifespan.
• Cycle effects on performance are small; individual variation is large. Energy
availability comes first.
• Women's products should be built on evidence, not trends.
• Industry can close the gap: fund, design and report better research
