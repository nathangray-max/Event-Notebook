# Fuelling the female runner: from paper to podium to partnerships

## Summary
James Morton (Science in Sport) argued that the biggest barrier to fuelling the female runner is educational, not biological [00:00] (photo 20). From a 2021 Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise study of recreational runners with muscle biopsies before and after three sessions, women started with less muscle glycogen than men but finished the same; the gap was under 5 g in the gastrocnemius and disappears when the same absolute carbohydrate is given, so it does not change practical guidance [00:00]. In an England Athletics study of elite runners (marathon bests about 2:20 for men and 2:40 for women) fed 60, 90 or 120 g of carbohydrate an hour over a two-hour run (15 minutes at 95% of lactate threshold, 90 minutes at 94% of lactate turnpoint, 15 minutes at 95% of lactate threshold), 120 g prevented the crossover from carbohydrate to fat in both sexes, exogenous oxidation reached about 1 g a minute at 60 g, 1.3 to 1.4 at 90 g and some individuals oxidised 80 to 100 percent of what they took at 120 g, and running economy improved by two to 3% in both sexes; the male data is Ravikanti et al. (2025) in the Journal of Applied Physiology and the female data is under revision [00:00] (photo 2) (photo 6) (photo 7). He showed a fuelling framework from Morton et al. (2026) in the Journal of Nutrition that sets carbohydrate by intensity and duration, from none for an easy hour to 120 g an hour dual source for a marathon, and described the SiS partnership with Nike's After Dark Tour as an education programme in seven cities with a co-branded race pack and a range "designed for her" [00:00] (photo 12) (photo 17) (photo 19). He does not believe in fuelling to the menstrual cycle: treat the female athlete as an athlete first [00:00] (photo 20). In the Q&A he said power analysis supports nine participants, fat-free mass may be the better dosing basis for women, and dual-source blends are needed at 90 and 120 g but not below 90 minutes [22:54], [23:48], [24:34], [25:17].

## Key takeaways
- In the 2021 study (recreational runners, 6 g per kg carbohydrate on the day, a 10-mile run at lactate threshold, 8 x 800 m, 3 x 10 minutes), women began with less muscle glycogen than men but both finished similarly depleted; the difference was less than 5 g in the gastrocnemius and has no practical bearing on guidelines [00:00].
- The gap is a dosing artefact: carbohydrate is prescribed per kilogram, so lighter women take less in absolute terms; given the same absolute amount, "there isn't really any differences in glycogen storage". His hedge: for a given relative intake women "may present with slightly less absolute glycogen" [00:00].
- Study design: 8 g per kg carbohydrate on day one, a 2 g per kg breakfast on day two, then 15 minutes at 95% of lactate threshold, 90 minutes at 94% of lactate turnpoint (simulated race pace) and 15 minutes at 95% of lactate threshold; the 60 g an hour condition was maltodextrin only, the 90 and 120 g conditions 1:1 maltodextrin and fructose [00:00] (photo 2).
- Elite runners (men about 2:20, women about 2:40 marathon bests) ran two hours after 8 g per kg the day before and 2 g per kg at breakfast; 60 g an hour gave a crossover to fat after about 60 minutes, 90 g shifted it right, 120 g prevented it for the full two hours [00:00]. The slides show the crossover at about 60 to 75 minutes on 60 g in both sexes and no crossover on 120 g in either, with men's carbohydrate oxidation on 120 g holding at about 3.0 to 3.2 g a minute from 45 to 105 minutes (photo 3) (photo 4) (photo 6).
- Exogenous oxidation (carbon-13): about 1 g a minute at 60 g an hour, close to 1.3 to 1.4 at 90 g, and at 120 g some men and women oxidised between 80 and 100 percent of intake, "some of the highest rates of oxidation ever reported"; men and women do not differ in ability to digest and oxidise carbohydrate [00:00]. The slide's peak means, read from the chart: men about 1.0, 1.5 and 1.8 g a minute at 60, 90 and 120 g; women about 1.0, 1.45 and 1.75; seven to eight participants a group; "120 > 90 > 60 g" in both sexes (photo 7).
- Running economy improved by between two and 3% at 120 g an hour, against Nike's 4% Vaporfly claim; he is "pretty sure" this is part of why running records are falling [00:00]. The conclusion slide: 120 g an hour "reduces the oxygen cost of running (i.e. improves running economy) compared with traditional CHO guidelines of 60-90 g/h" (photo 9).
- Women reported more gastrointestinal symptoms than men, "which may be due to anatomical differences in colon length"; tolerance may differ slightly, oxidation does not [00:00]. The slide's wording: "individual variations in rates of exogenous CHO oxidation and gastrointestinal symptoms warrant CHO personalisation strategies" (photo 9).
- Fuelling matrix (Journal of Nutrition): an easy 60-minute run needs no carbohydrate; 60 minutes at higher intensity 30 to 60 g an hour; team sport or half marathon towards 90 g; two to three hours a definite rationale for 120 g. Ultra-endurance (three to eight hours) is the gap SiS's science strategy will work on [00:00].
- The framework in full (Morton et al. 2026, Journal of Nutrition) plots intensity (zones 1 to 5; moderate under 2 mmol/L, heavy 2 to 4, severe over 4) against duration: under an hour, 0 g, 0 to 30 g or 30 to 60 g single source for HIT (mouth rinse, drinks, gels); one to two hours, 30 to 60 g single source, 60 to 90 g single or dual source for soccer, 90 g dual source for a half marathon (drinks, gels, chews); two to three hours, 60 to 90 g single or dual, 90 to 120 g dual, 120 g dual source for a marathon (drinks, gels, chews, bars); beyond three hours cycling stages, beyond six ultra (photo 12).
- The barriers: female marathoners "do not know the numbers" (psychological capability; participant 18 took her first gel at 7 or 8 miles and went by what others do) and tolerating carbohydrate during a race can be a barrier (physical capability; participant 13 aimed for about 80 g an hour, felt her stomach "was not happy" in the heat and skipped her last two gels); like men, female runners need to practise fuelling in training (photo 13) (photo 14) (photo 15).
- SiS partnered with Nike After Dark (women's night races in seven cities) on education rather than product creation, with co-branded products by race duration; the partnership continues [00:00]. The Sydney 21K race pack sells at $40.00 ("Fuel your night"): eight packs of Run Fuel Endurance energy gels (3 x 60 ml), Run Hydro+ electrolyte powder (4.3 g), a Run Fuel Endurance energy chew (50 g) and Run Recovery Endurance powder (73 g) (photo 17).
- The Nike x SiS After Dark range, "designed for her", laid out for 10K and half marathon: Run Fuel Isotonic gel (22 g single-source carbohydrate; before and during), Run Fuel Endurance gel (40 g dual source; during), Run Fuel Endurance chew (40 g dual source, 1:0.8 glucose to fructose; before and during), Run Hydro+ sugar-free electrolytes (350 mg sodium; before, during and after), Run Recovery (25 g single-source carbohydrate, 25 g protein including 5 g collagen; after) and Run Recovery Endurance (40 g dual-source carbohydrate, 25 g protein including 5 g collagen; after) (photo 19).
- "The biggest barrier to fueling the female runner is not biological... it is educational"; under-fuelling is cultural; he does not believe in day-of-cycle fuelling messages [00:00]. The closing slide: the barrier "is not likely a biological limitation i.e. their gut or their muscles" but "lack of knowledge and awareness of the guidelines", in "a world where under-fuelling has been prevalent"; fuelling "is less about hormones and tailoring to the menstrual cycle, put simply, it is about fuelling for the work required" (photo 20).
- Q&A: nine participants gives 80% power because a 30 g step changes oxidation by 0.3 to 0.4 g a minute [22:54]; women should probably be dosed per kilogram of fat-free mass, but it is hard to measure [23:48]; 60 g was maltodextrin only, 90 and 120 g dual source; dual source is needed above about 90 minutes and lowers oxidation at 60 g [24:34], [25:17].

## For Vita Actives
Nothing in the talk touches our ingredients: no protein, creatine or pre-workout was mentioned from the stage, and the only protein on a slide is the 25 g (including 5 g collagen) in the two Run Recovery drinks of the Nike x SiS range, source not stated (photo 19). The value is customer insight: SiS builds its pitch on its own trials (England Athletics, Nike), published papers and a one-slide framework, and its chief science voice is openly sceptical of claims he sees on social media [00:00] (photo 12). My reading is that a sports nutrition brand of this kind expects a supplier to bring trial data and a one-slide rule of thumb, not a benefit list, and that is the standard CreaWin and IsoClr: Pea material should meet. The gut tolerance point (more GI symptoms in women at high intakes, and a runner skipping gels on an unhappy stomach) is adjacent to the digestibility story for a clear pea protein (photo 15) (my reading). The clear recovery drinks carry 25 g protein in a clear format for women runners, which is the format IsoClr: Pea is made for (my reading). Relevance low: carbohydrate fuelling, outside our range.

- "When we consume 120 grams of carbohydrate per hour, not only have you shifted the crossover point, you've completely prevented it." Speaker 1 (James Morton) [00:00]
- "We get an improvement in running economy between two and 3%, depending on sexes, and so I'm I'm pretty sure that that's why you are seeing a lot of these running records and performances improve." Speaker 1 (James Morton) [00:00]
- "I would say that the biggest buyer to fueling the female runner is not biological. They don't have special guts or special muscles that we need to consider. Actually, I would say the biggest buyer, like men actually as well, is educational." Speaker 1 (James Morton) [00:00]
- "I'd rather be in the approximately right count than the precisely wrong count." Speaker 1 (James Morton) [23:48]
- "Female trained runners can oxidise exogenous CHO at comparable rates to male runners. Furthermore, in both sexes, 120 g/h maintains whole body CHO oxidation, CHO dependency and reduces the oxygen cost of running (i.e. improves running economy) compared with traditional CHO guidelines of 60-90 g/h. However, individual variations in rates of exogenous CHO oxidation and gastrointestinal symptoms warrant CHO personalisation strategies." Slide (photo 9)
- "The biggest barrier to fuelling the female runner is not likely a biological limitation i.e. their gut or their muscles. Rather, it is lack of knowledge and awareness of the guidelines (i.e. educational) and the associated physiological and performance benefits, especially when considering a world where under-fuelling has been prevalent. Fuelling is less about hormones and tailoring to the menstrual cycle, put simply, it is about fuelling for the work required." Slide (photo 20)
