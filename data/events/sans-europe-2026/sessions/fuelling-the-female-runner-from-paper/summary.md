# Fuelling the female runner: from paper to podium to partnerships

## Summary
James Morton of Science in Sport asked whether women need to fuel differently from men when they run. His answer: the barrier is knowledge, not biology.

He started with glycogen. In a 2021 study of recreational runners, women began with less muscle glycogen than men but finished equally depleted. The gap came from dosing per kilogram of body weight: given the same absolute carbohydrate, women store about the same.

He then showed an England Athletics study of elite runners fed 60, 90 or 120 g of carbohydrate an hour over two hours near race pace. At 120 g the switch from carbohydrate to fat never came, in men or in women. Both sexes oxidised the extra carbohydrate equally well, and running economy improved by two to 3%. Women reported more gut symptoms, so tolerance may differ slightly, but oxidation does not.

From this he drew a framework that sets carbohydrate by intensity and duration, up to 120 g an hour for a marathon, and described SiS's partnership with Nike's After Dark Tour as an education programme rather than a product launch. His conclusion: female runners do not know the numbers and have lived with under-fuelling. Treat the female athlete as an athlete first, and fuel for the work, not the menstrual cycle.

## Key takeaways
- Women started with less muscle glycogen than men but finished similarly depleted. The gap was under 5 g in the gastrocnemius, comes from dosing per kilogram, and has no practical bearing on guidelines [00:00].
- At 120 g of carbohydrate an hour, the crossover from carbohydrate to fat was completely prevented over two hours, in both sexes; at 60 g it came after about an hour [00:00] (photo 4) (photo 6).
- Men and women oxidise carbohydrate taken during exercise at comparable rates, rising with dose (120 > 90 > 60 g); at 120 g some individuals oxidised 80 to 100 percent of what they took [00:00] (photo 7).
- Running economy improved by two to 3% at 120 g an hour, against the 4% Nike claimed for the Vaporfly; he is "pretty sure" this is part of why running records are falling [00:00] (photo 9).
- Women reported more gastrointestinal symptoms, "which may be due to anatomical differences in colon length"; individual variation warrants personalised fuelling [00:00] (photo 9).
- The biggest barrier is educational, in a world where under-fuelling has been prevalent; he does not believe in fuelling to the menstrual cycle [00:00] (photo 20).

## Detail
- The 2021 study (Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise): recreational runners with muscle biopsies before and after three sessions, on 6 g per kg carbohydrate on the day, a 10-mile run at lactate threshold, 8 x 800 m and 3 x 10 minutes [00:00].
- Given the same absolute carbohydrate, "there isn't really any differences in glycogen storage", even if women then take eight or nine grams per kilogram. His hedge: for a given relative intake women "may present with slightly less absolute glycogen" [00:00].
- The elite study: men with marathon bests about 2:20, women about 2:40. 8 g per kg carbohydrate on day one, a 2 g per kg breakfast on day two, then 15 minutes at 95% of lactate threshold, 90 minutes at 94% of lactate turnpoint (simulated race pace) and 15 minutes at 95% of lactate threshold. The 60 g an hour condition was maltodextrin only; 90 and 120 g were 1:1 maltodextrin and fructose [00:00] (photo 2). The male data is Ravikanti et al. (2025), Journal of Applied Physiology; the female data is under revision [00:00].
- Crossover: at 60 g an hour, after about 60 minutes (the slides show about 60 to 75 minutes in both sexes); 90 g shifted it right; 120 g prevented it for the full two hours. Men's carbohydrate oxidation on 120 g held at about 3.0 to 3.2 g a minute from 45 to 105 minutes [00:00] (photo 3) (photo 4) (photo 6).
- Exogenous oxidation (carbon-13): about 1 g a minute at 60 g an hour, close to 1.3 to 1.4 at 90 g, and at 120 g "some of the highest rates of oxidation ever reported in literature". Peak means read from the chart: men about 1.0, 1.5 and 1.8 g a minute at 60, 90 and 120 g; women about 1.0, 1.45 and 1.75; seven to eight participants a group [00:00] (photo 7).
- The conclusion slide: "Female trained runners can oxidise exogenous CHO at comparable rates to male runners. Furthermore, in both sexes, 120 g/h maintains whole body CHO oxidation, CHO dependency and reduces the oxygen cost of running (i.e. improves running economy) compared with traditional CHO guidelines of 60-90 g/h. However, individual variations in rates of exogenous CHO oxidation and gastrointestinal symptoms warrant CHO personalisation strategies." (photo 9)
- Women may differ slightly in their ability to tolerate carbohydrate at those intensities; there is "definitely not a difference" in their ability to digest and oxidise it [00:00].
- The fuelling matrix (Journal of Nutrition): an easy 60-minute run needs no carbohydrate; 60 minutes at higher intensity 30 to 60 g an hour; team sport or half marathon towards 90 g; two to three hours a definite rationale for 120 g. Ultra-endurance (three to eight hours) is the gap SiS's science strategy will work on [00:00].
- The framework in full (Morton et al. 2026, Journal of Nutrition) plots intensity (zones 1 to 5; moderate under 2 mmol/L, heavy 2 to 4, severe over 4) against duration: under an hour, 0 g, 0 to 30 g or 30 to 60 g single source for HIT (mouth rinse, drinks, gels); one to two hours, 30 to 60 g single source, 60 to 90 g single or dual source for soccer, 90 g dual source for a half marathon (drinks, gels, chews); two to three hours, 60 to 90 g single or dual, 90 to 120 g dual, 120 g dual source for a marathon (drinks, gels, chews, bars); beyond three hours cycling stages, beyond six ultra (photo 10) (photo 12).
- The barriers: female marathoners "do not know the numbers" (psychological capability; participant 18 took her first gel at 7 or 8 miles and went by what others do), and tolerating carbohydrate in a race can be a barrier (physical capability; participant 13 aimed for about 80 g an hour, felt her stomach "was not happy" in the heat and skipped her last two gels). Like men, female runners need to practise fuelling in training (photo 13) (photo 14) (photo 15).
- Nike's After Dark Tour runs women's night races in seven cities. SiS partnered on education rather than product creation, with co-branded products by race duration; the partnership continues [00:00]. The Sydney 21K race pack sells at $40.00 ("Fuel your night"): eight packs of Run Fuel Endurance energy gels (3 x 60 ml), Run Hydro+ electrolyte powder (4.3 g), a Run Fuel Endurance energy chew (50 g) and Run Recovery Endurance powder (73 g) (photo 17) (photo 18).
- The Nike x SiS After Dark range, "designed for her", laid out for 10K and half marathon: Run Fuel Isotonic gel (22 g single-source carbohydrate; before and during), Run Fuel Endurance gel (40 g dual source; during), Run Fuel Endurance chew (40 g dual source, 1:0.8 glucose to fructose; before and during), Run Hydro+ sugar-free electrolytes (350 mg sodium; before, during and after), Run Recovery (25 g single-source carbohydrate, 25 g protein including 5 g collagen; after) and Run Recovery Endurance (40 g dual-source carbohydrate, 25 g protein including 5 g collagen; after) (photo 19).
- The closing slide: "The biggest barrier to fuelling the female runner is not likely a biological limitation i.e. their gut or their muscles. Rather, it is lack of knowledge and awareness of the guidelines (i.e. educational) and the associated physiological and performance benefits, especially when considering a world where under-fuelling has been prevalent. Fuelling is less about hormones and tailoring to the menstrual cycle, put simply, it is about fuelling for the work required." (photo 20) From the stage he added that he does not believe in day-of-cycle fuelling at all, and treats a female athlete as an athlete first [00:00].
