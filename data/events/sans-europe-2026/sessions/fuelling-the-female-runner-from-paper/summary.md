# Fuelling the female runner: from paper to podium to partnerships

## Summary
James Morton (Science in Sport) argued that the biggest barrier to fuelling the female runner is educational, not biological [00:00]. From a 2021 Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise study of recreational runners with muscle biopsies before and after three sessions, women started with less muscle glycogen than men but finished the same; the gap was under 5 g in the gastrocnemius and disappears when the same absolute carbohydrate is given, so it does not change practical guidance [00:00]. In an England Athletics study of elite runners (marathon bests about 2:20 for men and 2:40 for women) fed 60, 90 or 120 g of carbohydrate an hour over a two-hour run, 120 g prevented the crossover from carbohydrate to fat, exogenous oxidation reached about 1 g a minute at 60 g, 1.3 to 1.4 at 90 g and some individuals oxidised 80 to 100 percent of what they took at 120 g, and running economy improved by two to 3% in both sexes; the female data is in second-stage peer review [00:00]. He showed a fuelling matrix from a Journal of Nutrition paper that sets carbohydrate by intensity and duration, from none for an easy hour to 120 g an hour for two to three hours, and described the SiS partnership with Nike After Dark as an education programme in seven cities [00:00]. He does not believe in fuelling to the menstrual cycle: treat the female athlete as an athlete first [00:00]. In the Q&A he said power analysis supports nine participants, fat-free mass may be the better dosing basis for women, and dual-source blends are needed at 90 and 120 g but not below 90 minutes [22:54], [23:48], [24:34], [25:17].

## Key takeaways
- In the 2021 study (recreational runners, 6 g per kg carbohydrate on the day, a 10-mile run at lactate threshold, 8 x 800 m, 3 x 10 minutes), women began with less muscle glycogen than men but both finished similarly depleted; the difference was less than 5 g in the gastrocnemius and has no practical bearing on guidelines [00:00].
- The gap is a dosing artefact: carbohydrate is prescribed per kilogram, so lighter women take less in absolute terms; given the same absolute amount, "there isn't really any differences in glycogen storage". His hedge: for a given relative intake women "may present with slightly less absolute glycogen" [00:00].
- Elite runners (men about 2:20, women about 2:40 marathon bests) ran two hours after 8 g per kg the day before and 2 g per kg at breakfast; 60 g an hour gave a crossover to fat after about 60 minutes, 90 g shifted it right, 120 g prevented it for the full two hours [00:00].
- Exogenous oxidation (carbon-13): about 1 g a minute at 60 g an hour, close to 1.3 to 1.4 at 90 g, and at 120 g some men and women oxidised between 80 and 100 percent of intake, "some of the highest rates of oxidation ever reported"; men and women do not differ in ability to digest and oxidise carbohydrate [00:00].
- Running economy improved by between two and 3% at 120 g an hour, against Nike's 4% Vaporfly claim; he is "pretty sure" this is part of why running records are falling [00:00].
- Women reported more gastrointestinal symptoms than men, "which may be due to anatomical differences in colon length"; tolerance may differ slightly, oxidation does not [00:00].
- Fuelling matrix (Journal of Nutrition): an easy 60-minute run needs no carbohydrate; 60 minutes at higher intensity 30 to 60 g an hour; team sport or half marathon towards 90 g; two to three hours a definite rationale for 120 g. Ultra-endurance (three to eight hours) is the gap SiS's science strategy will work on [00:00].
- SiS partnered with Nike After Dark (women's night races in seven cities) on education rather than product creation, with co-branded products by race duration; the partnership continues [00:00].
- "The biggest barrier to fueling the female runner is not biological... it is educational"; under-fuelling is cultural; he does not believe in day-of-cycle fuelling messages [00:00].
- Q&A: nine participants gives 80% power because a 30 g step changes oxidation by 0.3 to 0.4 g a minute [22:54]; women should probably be dosed per kilogram of fat-free mass, but it is hard to measure [23:48]; 60 g was maltodextrin only, 90 and 120 g dual source; dual source is needed above about 90 minutes and lowers oxidation at 60 g [24:34], [25:17].

## For Vita Actives
Nothing here touches our ingredients: no protein, creatine or pre-workout was mentioned. The value is customer insight: SiS builds its pitch on its own trials (England Athletics, Nike), published papers and a simple matrix, and its chief science voice is openly sceptical of claims he sees on social media [00:00]. My reading is that a sports nutrition brand of this kind expects a supplier to bring trial data and a one-slide rule of thumb, not a benefit list, and that is the standard CreaWin and IsoClr: Pea material should meet. The gut tolerance point (more GI symptoms in women at high intakes) is adjacent to the digestibility story for a clear pea protein (my reading). Relevance low: carbohydrate fuelling, outside our range.
