# Fuel the Frame: Carbohydrate, recovery and the forgotten tissue

## Summary
Dr Sharon Madigan (PhD, RD, FFSEM) started from the slide everyone knows: 1 to 1.2 g of carbohydrate per kg per hour for rapid recovery in the first four hours after exercise. That is correct, she said, but nowhere near the whole story.

Her case is that carbohydrate is a signal, not just fuel. When it is available, the body treats it as safe to invest in bone, iron handling, hormones, immunity and sleep. When it is short, those are switched off to protect muscle output. A glycogen debt shows up as heavy legs the next day. The other costs build silently for weeks, until they appear as a bone stress injury, low ferritin or menstrual dysfunction.

Bone, the forgotten tissue, responds to every session in the same window used to fuel muscle. Carbohydrate during running blunts a marker of bone breakdown, and enough energy overall can still leave bone at risk if carbohydrate is low. Back-loading food into the evening, training low and cutting weight carry the same hidden costs, and low carbohydrate also amplifies the hormone that blocks iron absorption after exercise.

She closed on the limits. Most bone-marker studies are in men and measure hours or weeks, not fractures, and the dose and timing that protect bone are not yet defined.

## Key takeaways
- Carbohydrate is "a Signal, Not Just a Substrate": its availability is "an electrical green light" telling the body it is metabolically safe to invest in bone remodelling, iron handling and endocrine function (slide 3) (slide 4).
- Quick against quiet costs: a glycogen deficit shows as a flat session and heavy legs, usually fixed within a day of good fuelling; bone, iron and hormones give no immediate signal, and by the time a bone stress injury, low ferritin or menstrual dysfunction appears the debt has been building for weeks (slide 5).
- Bone markers: carbohydrate during running significantly reduces the acute rise in the resorption marker CTX, and carbohydrate with protein after exercise alters bone turnover markers favourably; about 3.5 weeks of a ketogenic diet in race walkers raised resorption and suppressed formation, with incomplete recovery once carbohydrate was restored. The slide notes these are acute responses over hours, mostly in men (Sale et al., 2015) (slide 8).
- The hidden risk zone: short-term carbohydrate restriction suppresses bone formation more than low energy availability does, so energy intake alone will not flag bone risk and practitioners must screen for carbohydrate availability (Heikura et al., 2020; Fensham et al., 2022) (slide 10).
- Timing: even with an identical daily total, within-day energy deficiency is linked to unfavourable hormonal profiles in female and male athletes (Fahrenholtz et al., 2018); "Daily totals cannot save a back-loaded schedule" (slide 12) (slide 20).
- Iron: a hard endurance session triggers an IL-6 spike and hepcidin rises over 3-6 hours; low carbohydrate availability amplifies this, "functionally blocking iron absorption", so iron-rich meals and supplements should be timed around carbohydrate-restored windows (slide 13).

## Detail
- The deck's subtitle: "Clinical Insights on Carbohydrates as a Systemic Survival Signal" (slide 1). Every slide except 19 is an image; the text below is read from the slide images.
- The traditional view treats carbohydrate as "gas in the tank" to refill muscle glycogen when the next session is close (slide 2). Her paradigm shift: from substrate to signal ("Governor of systemic safety"); from muscle and liver to bone, endocrine, immune and iron handling; from flat sessions tomorrow to stress injuries and hormonal dysfunction next month; from daily totals to intra-day timing (slide 4).
- Glycogen: under 8 hours to the next session, start early and feed often, speed being the main variable; over 24 hours, total carbohydrate over the day matters more than precise timing. Glucose-fructose blends restore liver glycogen more effectively than glucose alone (Burke et al., 2011; Fuchs et al., 2019) (slide 6).
- "Bone responds to every single session": exercise shifts bone turnover in the hours afterwards, the same window used to fuel muscle (slide 7).
- On slide 8, β-CTX reflects existing bone being broken down and P1NP new bone being laid down; the footnote's second reference is cut off after "Townse" (slide 8).
- The blind spot: low energy availability is measured to prevent the Female Athlete Triad and RED-S, but low carbohydrate availability is a compounding variable. Read from the chart: the low-energy bar is mostly carbohydrate, the low-carb high-fat bar is tall with a thin carbohydrate layer; the bars carry no values (slide 9).
- Lodge et al. (2023) note that low carbohydrate availability "operates as an independent risk factor" within RED-S, overlapping on glycogen stores, bone health (stress injuries), menstrual function and immunity (slide 11).
- Within-day fuelling, read from the chart: targeted fuelling spreads intake across the day with short deficits; back-loaded fuelling eats little around training and catches up in the evening, with long, deep deficits. The curves are illustrative, without values (slide 12).
- The switchboard: when carbohydrate drops, power stays with heart, lungs and muscle output, and is cut to bone formation, iron handling, hormonal reproduction and immune defence; this also drives immune disturbance in heavy blocks and disrupts sleep onset and quality (slide 14).
- Her diagnostic matrix rates targeted fuelling optimal or protected on every line; back-loaded fuelling sub-optimal, with P1NP suppressed, β-CTX elevated and hormones and iron disrupted; train-low variable or compromised, with P1NP highly suppressed and β-CTX highly elevated (slide 15).
- Train low "heavily suppresses bone formation and increases injury risk without consistent performance benefits in elite racing"; repeated weight cuts mean repeated exposure to low carbohydrate availability over a season. Absolute contraindications for low-carbohydrate protocols: adolescent athletes, athletes with low energy availability or menstrual dysfunction, and athletes with a history of bone stress injuries (slide 16).
- Two more quiet costs: carbohydrate availability influences immune disturbance after prolonged heavy exercise (high risk in heavy blocks, major competitions and travel; Walsh, 2019), and evening carbohydrate influences sleep onset and quality (high risk with late training and evening competitions; Halson, 2014) (slide 17).
- Making weight: carbohydrate is cut before the weigh-in to reduce glycogen, water and gut content, followed by a panicked refeed limited by gut tolerance (slide 18).
- Where evidence runs thin: female athletes (mostly male bone-marker studies), adolescents (peak bone mass being built, little direct data), outcomes (markers, not fractures) and dose and timing (slide 19).
- Her four pillars: protect more than glycogen; fuel recovery early to avoid the within-day deficit; treat low carbohydrate availability as an independent bone-health risk even when calories look adequate; periodise with intent and keep adolescent, low-energy and previously injured athletes off train-low protocols (slide 20).
