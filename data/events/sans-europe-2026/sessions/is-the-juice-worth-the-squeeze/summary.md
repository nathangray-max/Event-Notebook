# Is the Juice Worth the Squeeze? Polyphenols in Performance and Recovery

## Summary
Mark Evans, a senior scientist at Holland & Barrett, asked whether polyphenols earn their place in sports nutrition. He began with a quiz. Protein, creatine and caffeine each have a known dose and timing. Polyphenols have general guidance too, but it leaves a question: milligrams of what?

Polyphenols are several thousand compounds, not one thing. The same figure on a label can mean a whole extract, a subclass or a single compound. They work less as antioxidants than as signals. Of the five ways they act, only raising blood flow through nitric oxide shows up consistently in people. Of the four best-studied sources, blackcurrant has the strongest evidence for performance and cherry for recovery.

His lesson came from outside polyphenols. Beetroot nitrate and a broccoli sprout extract built awareness by teaching a simple rule of use, not a mechanism. Brands are starting to name anthocyanins, and he asked whether consumers will learn the word.

His answer: for some polyphenols, yes. Cherry at over 1,000 mg a day for recovery and blackcurrant at about 300 mg before exercise are reasonably settled. The industry now has to standardise and declare what is in the product, fund better trials and communicate dose and timing.

## Key takeaways
- The dose problem: guidance from fruit-polyphenol studies is about 300 mg 1 to 2 hours before exercise for performance and over 1,000 mg a day for at least 3 days around hard exercise for recovery, but the slide asks "mg of what?" (slide 3). A label figure can mean total polyphenols, a subclass or one compound; his three questions for any ingredient are which compound, how much and measured how (slide 5).
- Mechanism: blood levels are too low for polyphenols to act as direct antioxidants; they act as signals. Of five mechanisms, only increased blood flow (nitric oxide) is rated "consistent" in humans; the others are rated limited, mostly animal, inconsistent or mostly lab (slide 6).
- The big four: blackcurrant has the strongest evidence for performance and cherry for recovery; pomegranate (performance) and turmeric (recovery) are equivocal; 3 of the 4 share anthocyanins as a main polyphenol (slide 7).
- The lesson from nitrate and Nomio: make the dose and timing the message, and let credible users do the teaching. Beetroot nitrate became "one shot, 2–3 hours before and/or for 1 week prior to competition" (slide 11).
- A layered label: "Rich in polyphenols" on the front for awareness, "X mg anthocyanins per serving, standardised and batch tested" on the back for credibility (slide 12).
- His three asks of the industry: standardise and declare, fund the right trials (trained athletes, women, blood levels, real outcomes) and communicate dose and timing (slide 13).

## Detail
- The quiz's reference doses: protein about 0.3 g/kg per meal (20 to 40 g) and about 1.6 g/kg a day for muscle gain, every 3 to 4 hours; creatine 3 to 5 g a day (or 20 g a day for 5 to 7 days to load), about 4 weeks to saturate muscle without loading; caffeine 3 to 6 mg/kg about 60 minutes before exercise (slide 2).
- Worked examples of the guidance: Montmorency cherry 600 mg twice daily for at least 3 days before and during recovery; pomegranate about 620 mg twice daily for 7 days before and after. Chronic dosing over 1 to 6 weeks showed no clear advantage over a single acute dose for performance. The source review (Bowtell and Kelly, 2019) appeared in a supplement sponsored by the Gatorade Sports Science Institute (slide 3).
- The four classes: flavonoids (anthocyanins, flavan-3-ols, flavonols such as quercetin, flavanones; the best active-nutrition evidence), phenolic acids, stilbenes (resveratrol) and lignans. Curcuminoids from turmeric do not sit neatly in any class (slide 4).
- Why label figures do not travel: quick "total phenolics" tests also react with vitamin C and can overstate content; variety, harvest and processing change the blend (cherry juice against powder); content varies by batch, so each batch should be tested; "standardised to X%" only helps if it says X% of what (slide 5).
- The five mechanisms by benefit: Nrf2 defences (recovery, limited in humans), blood flow (performance, consistent), new mitochondria (endurance, mostly animal), a shift towards fat as fuel (endurance, inconsistent), calming NF-κB inflammation (recovery, mostly lab) (slide 6).
- Ones to watch: cocoa flavanols improve blood vessel function (meta-analysis of 42 studies) and hold one of very few authorised polyphenol health claims, but 900 mg before a time trial gave no gain in trained cyclists; blueberry improves blood vessel function within about an hour at 0.3 to 1.9 g polyphenols, and 6 weeks of daily blueberries reduced oxidative stress after a 2.5-hour run, but few studies measure performance ("early: promising signals"); quercetin is easy to dose but human effects are small and inconsistent ("early: mostly animal data") (slide 8).
- Beyond sport, he sees strong signals and almost no consumer understanding: the first flavan-3-ol intake guideline (2022) and the cocoa flavanol claim for the heart; wild blueberry improved vascular function and cognition in older adults; most polyphenols reach the colon and interact with gut bacteria. The first to explain them well could lead well beyond sport (slide 9).
- What good evidence looks like: a standardised extract, total polyphenol and primary anthocyanin doses, trained athletes and women rather than recreational men, blood levels measured with outcomes, performance and strength recovery rather than biomarkers, and pre-registered, independent, properly powered trials (slide 10).
- Still open: what reaches the blood and does the work, the best dose for each source, and trials in women, older athletes and resistance training (slide 13).
- Nitrate history: University of Exeter research from around 2009 showed nitrate improved endurance efficiency, and products were standardised to a nitrate dose in a small shot. Nomio's sulforaphane is not a polyphenol but tells the same Nrf2 story; it built high awareness in its audience through endurance athletes and influencers, from a smaller evidence base. The slide cites Nomio as a communication example only (slide 11).
- Brand examples, read from the images: an AMACX cherry juice, a 500 ml ready-to-drink "improved formula" with 125 mg anthocyanins and 30 g carbohydrate at a 2:1 ratio; and a Flycarb "case study" post claiming a 5 km time trial 2.7% faster (2.8% in its body text), 37 seconds quicker, after a week of daily New Zealand blackcurrant (300 mg anthocyanins), with a comment imagining stacking Flycarb, ITC and anthocyanins. The slide shows them for illustration only, with no endorsement implied (slide 12).
