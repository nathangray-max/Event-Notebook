# Clicks to Community: Turning Digital Engagement into Brand Loyalty

## Summary
Nikki Hancocks (NutraIngredients) chaired a panel on how brands build communities online while regulators watch, opening with an NI report of more than 1,000 misleading food communication posts by influencers, supplements the most frequent subject [00:01]. Sinead Gibbons (Tonic Health) said 99% of Tonic's content is not about its products: the "Unsponsored Truth" supermarket swaps have built 1.9 million followers and about 70 million views a month, run by one 22-year-old social media manager and a freelance editor [01:00], [14:35]. Julian Harvie (AG1) said too much evidence fails at the top of the funnel, that AG1 practises "restraint", and that Clearcast rejected "the best version of you", "a better version" and "a slightly better version" before approving "Ever wondered what life would be like with a little extra help?" [04:17], [18:30]. Dan Richardson (nutritionist) said athletes want the headline, not the numbers, that buzzwords with different connotations from the research are the common slip, and that athletes are the most impressionable audience of all [06:30], [08:33], [32:33]. Luca Bucchini (for ESSNA) listed the rules (the nutrition and health claims regulation, the UK advertising codes, member state influencer rules, a new digital act coming) and the recurring failures: undisclosed ads, strategies built around non-compliance, and following non-compliant discourse; brands bear most responsibility for influencer claims [09:45], [11:59], [28:27]. On AI, Julian's rule is that the idea must begin and end with a human [35:43].

## Key takeaways
- More than 1,000 misleading food communication posts by influencers were counted in a recent report, with supplements the most frequent subject [00:01].
- Tonic's model is "a media business that happens to sell vitamins": 20 to 30 second clips, three posts a day, 1.9 million followers, about 70 million views a month, 99% of content not about the product; people come to social "to be educated or to be entertained" [01:00], [03:24].
- Tonic posts 900 pieces of content a month across four channels with a team of one social media manager and a freelance editor; after the ASA pulled up a profile cover claiming "the best sleep supplement in the market" for its magnesium drink, every element now goes through an AI compliance agent [14:35].
- Tonic never deletes a challenged post: it changes the caption, answers the comments, sends voice notes and makes response videos, because "trust is really built in those small moments" [14:35].
- AG1 found that talking about gold-standard trials and third-party testing did not land: "people didn't care". Too much evidence does not work top of funnel; the science sits mid-funnel for people in the consideration set [04:17], [18:30].
- Clearcast rejected "the best version of you", "a better version of you" and "a slightly better version of you"; the approved line was "Ever wondered what life would be like with a little extra help?" Julian's lesson: "creative constraint is a very powerful way to deliver better communication" [18:30].
- AG1 cannot associate healthcare professionals, dietitians or doctors directly with the brand, so it uses them for pure education ("what is creatine? Like, how does it work?", "why is magnesium important?") and hopes the credibility transfers [22:46].
- Dan: athletes want the headline backed by research; the common slip is swapping dull research words for buzzwords whose connotations differ, often "genuine accidental misleading"; "what you can and can't say around a product is a massive gray area" [06:30], [08:33].
- Dan is food first: he told a creatine company's podcast audience that not everybody should take creatine until the baseline of nutrition is fixed, and the company objected [24:22].
- Athletes are "probably the most impressionable group": a 35-year-old EFL player with 200-plus appearances bought a supplement off TikTok Shop; they believe "up to 10% better performance" applies to them personally [32:33].
- Luca: the rules are the nutrition and health claims regulation, the UK advertising codes, member state influencer rules and a new digital act whose text is not yet known; failures are undisclosed ads, no training or allocated responsibility with creators, strategies built around non-compliance, and following a non-compliant discourse [09:45], [11:59].
- Brands bear most responsibility for influencers' health claims in most jurisdictions; the answer is training, contracts and briefing [28:27].
- Provocation without a solution is fearmongering: Tonic will not post a call-out unless it gives the healthier swap [30:18].
- AI: Julian's hard rule is that "the idea has to begin and end with a human being"; AI scales the middle; he found human copy still better by some margin. Luca expects regulatory action as consumers take nutrition advice from AI agents; AI is also used by regulators to monitor posts [35:43], [38:28], [39:48].

## For Vita Actives
Our customers' marketing teams are under claims pressure from the ASA, Clearcast and the claims regulation, and the panel's answer was education content that does not sell: "what is creatine, how does it work" is exactly what AG1 makes with its experts [22:46]. My reading is that a branded ingredient earns its place by giving brands substantiated, claim-safe education they can publish, in the headline-plus-evidence form Dan described, not by giving them stronger claims. For CreaWin, Dan's food-first framing (creatine after the baseline is fixed) and the athlete anecdotes show creatine is already a mainstay in the eyes of athletes and nutritionists [24:22], [32:33]; my reading is that our creatine content should be calm and educational, and wording we supply to brands must survive an ASA review. Luca's point that brands carry the liability for what influencers say means brands will want suppliers' claims to be referenced and hedged exactly [28:27]. Relevance medium: marketing and regulation, adjacent to our ingredients.
