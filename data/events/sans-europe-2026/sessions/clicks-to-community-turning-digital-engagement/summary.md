# Clicks to Community: Turning Digital Engagement into Brand Loyalty

## Summary
Nikki Hancocks of NutraIngredients chaired a panel on how brands build communities online while regulators watch. She opened with a report of more than 1,000 misleading food posts by influencers, with supplements the most frequent subject.

The panel agreed that social content works when it educates or entertains rather than sells. Sinead Gibbons said almost none of Tonic Health's content is about its products; its supermarket swap videos have built 1.9 million followers. Julian Harvie said AG1 learned that talking about its trials and testing did not land, so it keeps the science further down the funnel and practises restraint; Clearcast rejected three versions of its TV advert's opening line. Dan Richardson, a nutritionist in professional sport, said athletes want the headline backed by research, that brands slip by using buzzwords whose meaning differs from the research, and that athletes are the most impressionable audience of all.

Luca Bucchini, speaking for ESSNA, set out the rules and the failures he sees most: undisclosed ads, and marketing strategies built around non-compliance. Brands carry most of the responsibility for what their influencers claim. On AI, Julian's rule is that every idea begins and ends with a human; Luca expects regulators to act as people take nutrition advice from AI.

## Key takeaways
- More than 1,000 misleading food posts by influencers were counted in a recent report, with supplements the most frequent subject [00:01].
- Tonic runs itself as "a media business that happens to sell vitamins": 99% of its content is not about its products, and it has 1.9 million followers and about 70 million views a month [01:00], [03:24].
- AG1 found that talking about trials and third-party testing did not land: "people didn't care". Too much evidence fails at the top of the funnel; the science sits mid-funnel [04:17], [18:30].
- Clearcast rejected "the best version of you", "a better version of you" and "a slightly better version of you" before approving "Ever wondered what life would be like with a little extra help?" Julian's lesson: "creative constraint is a very powerful way to deliver better communication" [18:30].
- Brands bear most responsibility for their influencers' health claims in most jurisdictions; the answer is training, contracts and briefing [28:27].
- Athletes are "probably the most impressionable group": they believe a claim of "up to 10% better performance" applies to them personally [32:33].

## For Vita Actives
- Our customers' marketing teams are under claims pressure from the ASA, Clearcast and the claims regulation, and the panel's answer was education that does not sell: "what is creatine, how does it work" is exactly what AG1 makes with its experts [22:46].
- My reading: a branded ingredient earns its place by giving brands substantiated, claim-safe education they can publish, in the headline-plus-evidence form Dan described, not by giving them stronger claims [06:30].
- For CreaWin, Dan's food-first framing and his athlete stories show creatine is already a mainstay for athletes and nutritionists [24:22], [32:33]. My reading: our creatine content should be calm and educational, and wording we supply to brands must survive an ASA review.
- Brands carry the liability for what influencers say [28:27]. My reading: they will want suppliers' claims referenced and hedged exactly.
- Relevance medium: marketing and regulation, adjacent to our ingredients.

## Detail
- Tonic's flagship content is the "Unsponsored Truth": its founder gives simple supermarket swaps. Clips are 20 to 30 seconds, three posts a day; people come to social "to be educated or to be entertained" [01:00], [03:24].
- Tonic posts 900 pieces of content a month across four channels with one 22-year-old social media manager and a freelance editor. After the ASA pulled up a profile cover for its magnesium drink ("...she wrote on the social cover the best sleep supplement in the market, and now we don't have that claim"), every piece now goes through an AI compliance agent [14:35].
- Tonic never deletes a challenged post: it changes the caption, answers the comments, sends voice notes and makes response videos, because "trust is really built in those small moments" [14:35].
- Julian: on social, people "come to be educated or entertained", and education must be hyper relevant to the person; "Too much evidence doesn't work." AG1's US campaign "WTF is NSF" and its "Dropping Hours" series humanise the science without selling [04:17].
- AG1 cannot associate healthcare professionals, dietitians or doctors directly with the brand, so it uses them for pure education ("what is creatine? Like, how does it work?", "why is magnesium important?") and hopes the credibility transfers [22:46].
- Dan: athletes want the headline, backed by the research. The common slip is swapping dull research words for buzzwords whose connotations differ, often "genuine accidental misleading"; "what you can and can't say around a product is a massive gray area" [06:30], [08:33].
- Dan is food first: on a creatine company's podcast he said not everybody should take creatine until the baseline of nutrition is fixed, and the company objected [24:22].
- A 35-year-old EFL player with 200-plus appearances bought a supplement off TikTok Shop; athletes think they are the subject of the research papers [32:33].
- Luca's rules: the nutrition and health claims regulation, the UK advertising codes, member state influencer rules, and a new digital act whose text is not yet known (Otter: "Digital Finance Act") [09:45].
- Luca's recurring failures: undisclosed ads, no training or allocated responsibility with creators, strategies built around non-compliance, and following a non-compliant discourse [11:59].
- Provocation without a solution is fearmongering: Tonic will not post a call-out unless it gives the healthier swap [30:18].
- AI: Julian's hard rule is that "...the idea has to begin and end with a human being"; AI scales the middle, and he found human copy still better by some margin [35:43]. Luca expects regulatory action as consumers take nutrition advice from AI agents [38:28]. The chair added that AI is also used to monitor posts [39:48].
