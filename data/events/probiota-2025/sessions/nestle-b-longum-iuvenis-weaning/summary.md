# Bifidobacterium longum subsp. iuvenis thrives around weaning and is adapted to transition from milk to solid diets

## Summary
Olga Sakwinska of Nestlé Research presented the discovery of a new subspecies of a common infant gut bacterium. Their starting point: the gut microbiome of early life shapes later health, from growth and metabolism to infections and vaccine response.

Bifidobacteria dominate the infant gut, and one subspecies, B. longum subsp. infantis, is built to feed on the sugars in breast milk. Nestlé followed 220 infants in Dhaka, Bangladesh, from birth to two years, with stool samples and metagenomic data. In that data a third group of B. longum turned up that was neither the infant nor the adult type. It peaked between about 10 and 24 months, in children eating both breast milk and solid food.

The group was common in Bangladesh, at about 61%, and rare elsewhere. Nestlé isolated five strains, read their genomes and found they can digest both breast-milk sugars and food glycans. The group was named a new subspecies, B. longum subsp. iuvenis, in 2023.

Their conclusion: B. l. iuvenis thrives around weaning and is adapted to the move from milk to solid food. The deck shows no product and no human trial of the strain.

## Key takeaways
- A transitional clade of B. longum, distinct from the infant (infantis) and adult (longum) types, was found in the metagenomes of infants in a Nestlé cohort in Bangladesh (slide 8).
- It is associated with partial breastfeeding, a diet "combining human milk and solid foods" (slide 9).
- It was present at 61.21% in Bangladesh (n=1,606) but at 0% to 18.18% in eight other countries' cohorts, including 0.50% in the USA (n=400) (slide 10).
- Its five sequenced strains carry enzymes for both breast-milk sugars (fucosylated HMOs) and diet-derived glycans (slide 11) (slide 12).
- Named a new subspecies in 2023, B. longum subsp. iuvenis, type strain NCC 5000T; isolates come from Asia and Africa (Modesto et al., 2023) (slide 13).

## Detail
- Early-life microbiome and health, four papers cited: lasting effects on health (Tamburini et al., Nature Medicine 2016), paediatric metabolic disorders (Jian et al., EBioMedicine 2021), undernutrition (Barratt et al., Cell Host & Microbe 2022) and vaccine response (Jordan et al., Lancet Microbe 2022) (slide 2).
- HMOs: more than 160 structures, 20 of them over 80% of the total; mostly not digested or absorbed; far more concentrated and varied in human milk than in farm animals' or pets' milk (slide 3).
- The cohort: Nestlé Microbiota and Health study, Dhaka, Bangladesh, 2013 to 2016 (NCT02361164), with icddr,b and the Broad Institute; n=220; growth, diarrhoea and respiratory infections, medicines and breastfeeding recorded; stool and nasopharynx samples at visits from birth to 24 months (slide 5).
- In the cohort most infants were breastfed and 25% were born by caesarean section; exclusive breastfeeding gave way to partial breastfeeding by about four months, and infection rates peaked between about 4 and 12 months, read from the chart (slide 6).
- Microbial diversity rose from birth to 24 months, and Bifidobacterium dominated absolute abundance from 2 to 15 months before Prevotella and others grew, read from the chart (Vatanen et al., Cell 2022) (slide 7).
- Relative to all B. longum, infantis was near 100% at 2 to 6 months and fell to near zero by 24 months; the transitional clade rose from about 10 months, with a median near 60% at 18 months, read from the chart (slide 8).
- Prevalence by cohort: Bangladesh 61.21%, China 18.18% (n=11), Italy 9.49%, Russia 5.19%, Estonia 3.61%, Sweden 1.33%, New Zealand 1.08%, USA 0.50%, Finland 0.00% (slide 10).
- Isolation: metagenomics flagged samples rich in the clade; 77 Bifidobacterium isolates were taken, 54 of them B. longum, 9 transitional; complete genomes gave 5 unique transitional strains (slide 11).
- In vitro, B. l. iuvenis and B. l. infantis grew on 2'-FL, 3-FL, diFL and LNT; B. l. longum and B. l. suis did not, read from the chart (Modesto et al., 2023) (slide 14).
- Like infantis, and unlike suis, B. l. iuvenis carries an operon to make riboflavin (slide 13).
- All figures are as presented in February 2025; the papers behind them date from 2016 to 2023. No slide is marked Confidential.
