# Postbiotics: a classification for industry and regulators

## Summary
Simone Guglielmetti opened the first day with the postbiotics roadmap that the International Probiotics Association (IPA) has coordinated. Their starting point is confusion. Academic definitions disagree, and products sold as postbiotics range from heat-killed cells to metabolites and even sodium butyrate. For the talk Guglielmetti used a broad working definition: any non-viable agent of microbial origin that confers a physiological benefit.

The roadmap is meant for commercial and regulatory use, which academic definitions do not address. It was reworked after about 400 stakeholder comments received in July. Its decision tree asks, in order: is it alive, was it inactivated on purpose, is it meant for food and supplements rather than disease, and is the parent microbe safe or approved? Purified molecules are now left out. What remains falls into four groups: microbial metabolites, intact cells, fragmented cells and complex mixtures. Guglielmetti placed real products in each, with pasteurised Akkermansia as the intact-cell case.

Their aim is to stop postbiotics being seen as a marketing gimmick. The paper was in draft. In questions Guglielmetti said the IPA kept the word "postbiotic" because the market uses it, that yeast extract would count, and that the evidence needed for a benefit depends on each regulator.

## Key takeaways
- Definitions clash: the academic one Guglielmetti cites covers inactivated cells and fragments but excludes metabolites, while products sold online use the word for metabolites, sodium butyrate and heat-killed cells [00:00].
- The IPA-coordinated roadmap was revised after "around 400 different specific comments from stakeholders", mostly companies, received in July [00:00].
- The decision tree's gates: not alive (probiotics and phages are out), intentional and reproducible inactivation, food or supplement use rather than treating disease, and a safe or approved parent microbe [00:00].
- Purified molecules (a metabolite, an enzyme, butyrate, beta-glucans) are now outside the category, one of the biggest changes made [00:00].
- Four groups remain: microbial metabolites (MM), intact cells (IC), fragmented cells (FC) and complex (CX), with combinations counted as complex [00:00].
- In questions Guglielmetti said the word "postbiotic" was reinstated because companies use it widely, and that excluding metabolites would mostly create confusion [17:15].

## Detail
- Their examples from an e-commerce search: metabolites produced by probiotics, sodium butyrate, heat-killed cells, and metabolites made by probiotics grown on prebiotics [00:00].
- The work began two to three years earlier (Guglielmetti gives both), was first shown in June at what Otter renders as the "IPC" probiotic conference, and drew on IPA members, regulators and governments' views [00:00].
- A parent microbe without a long history of safe use (not on the EU QPS list) can still be used once the final ingredient is approved by a competent authority [00:00].
- Akkermansia example: a product with pasteurised Akkermansia at 30 million units counted by flow cytometry; Guglielmetti says the live microbe had a negative QPS opinion in 2020 and a positive EFSA novel-food opinion a year later. Intact-cell group [00:00].
- Other examples: a lysate of lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria (fragmented cells); a fermented skim milk ingredient, heat-treated, with no separation from the medium (complex); hyaluronic acid purified from a non-food microbe (not a postbiotic); a cell-free product fermented by a paracasei strain (microbial metabolites) [00:00].
- All the groups still need notification or approval for efficacy; Guglielmetti did not cover efficacy [00:00].
- The roadmap paper was being finalised for publication [00:00].
- Yeast extract made in a standardised, reproducible way with a shown health benefit would, in their opinion, be a fragmented-cell postbiotic [18:50].
- On the burden of evidence Guglielmetti said they could not answer; it depends on regulators, and Europe's health-claim rules make it complex [19:32].
- Unfiltered beer with dead yeast could, in their opinion, be a food containing a postbiotic if standardised and a benefit shown, though it contains alcohol [20:12].
