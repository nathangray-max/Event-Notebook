# Gut-brain axis panel discussion

## Summary
Stephen Daniells of NutraIngredients chaired the Day 1 gut-brain panel after the break. Siobhain O'Mahony of APC Microbiome Ireland and Julia Rode of Örebro University had spoken before it; Katie Guzzetta of ETH Zurich and Emily Prpa, head of science at Yakult UK and Ireland, joined them. Daniells's question: how far does the science on gut microbes and the brain really go?

The panel agreed it is early. Most human evidence comes from clinical groups, such as people with depression, where probiotics helped as an add-on to treatment rather than on their own. Data on cognition in humans are sparse, and trials in healthy people are few, short and measure small shifts on subjective scales. Prpa said nutritional psychiatry is only about 50 years old and that probiotics are food, not medicine.

On products, nobody could say whether single strains or blends work better, and O'Mahony said formulators are limited to the few strains that are stable and safe. Prebiotics may need the right bacteria present first, which points to synbiotics.

Guzzetta brought mouse work: faecal transplants from young to old mice improved memory, and gut-made acetate reached the brain. Guzzetta warned that gut-made serotonin or GABA probably does not reach the brain. The recording ends as the panel turned to athletes' microbiomes.

## Key takeaways
- Most of the evidence is in clinical populations, with only a handful of studies in healthy people, and what counts as a good enough outcome in a healthy person is still unclear (Emily Prpa) [1:03:46].
- In bipolar and major depressive disorder, probiotics "can be well effective when used as an adjunct, not when administered on their own"; Prpa said translating that to the everyday person is not there yet, and randomised trials run about 12 weeks at most [1:08:40].
- Cognition in humans has little data; probiotics are the best studied, "But also there, it's quite sparse", and repeat testing makes change hard to capture (Julia Rode) [1:06:34] [1:07:09]. The panel asked whether prevention, before disease, is the window to act [1:07:33] [1:08:00].
- Single strain or multi-strain cannot be called: the studies differ in products, doses and durations (Rode) [1:11:44]; O'Mahony finds the single-strain evidence compelling but says formulators can only use the few strains that are stable and safe [1:12:47].
- A single prebiotic such as inulin may fail in depressed people whose gut lacks the bacteria to make short-chain fatty acids; Prpa suggested the gut may need conditioning first [1:15:27]. Guzzetta said a synbiotic may be needed [1:14:52].
- Mechanisms remain open: gut-made neurotransmitters probably do not reach the brain (Guzzetta) [1:19:11], and the effect is "definitely not only inflammation" (Rode) [1:26:21].

## Detail
- Sex differences are under-studied, even in animals; Guzzetta called this "really, really critical", and O'Mahony said it would help male-based therapies too [1:02:53] [1:03:32].
- In rodents there is a decent amount of data linking microbes to memory; in humans, O'Mahony said longitudinal studies going back to the years before symptoms are needed [1:05:06].
- Prpa: nutritional psychiatry, food or supplements as an adjunct or alternative to treatments such as antidepressants, is "like 50 years young"; a one-point drop on a subjective stress scale may be statistically significant but not felt [1:08:40].
- O'Mahony said they are developing a women's health supplement; half the strains first chosen were not stable or possible to put into a capsule, so fibres are used to support the bacteria wanted [1:14:15].
- Prpa: probiotics show strength in depression; for cognition there is "some interesting data out there" on relational memory; prebiotics show their strength in cognition and memory [1:15:27].
- Guzzetta: preclinical work in mice found that phages isolated from faeces and given back after stress reverted the stress response, so the virome may also be a target [1:16:54].
- Guzzetta: about 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut but probably does not change systemic levels; whether tryptophan- or serotonin-producing bacteria act through those metabolites is unclear [1:19:11].
- Guzzetta: a 2021 mouse study traced carbon-13 labelled acetate from the gut to the brain, where it entered microglia [1:21:23].
- Guzzetta's own study: faecal transplants from young to aged mice improved long-term memory and changed 35 metabolites in the hippocampus; Guzzetta pointed to Sean Gibbons's talk the next day on ageing trajectories (sean-gibbons-ageing-gut-microbiome) [1:22:59].
- Two case studies in Alzheimer's patients reported improved scores after faecal transplants, with follow-up to 12 weeks in one; Guzzetta noted the risks, and that many transplant trials were halted [1:24:41] [1:25:40].
- Rode and Guzzetta: short-chain fatty acids may affect blood-brain barrier permeability in cell culture, and act through immune cells that reach the brain [1:26:21] [1:27:00].
- Daniells asked whether, as with elite athletes and centenarians, special strains could be mined for psychobiotics [1:27:13].
