# Postbiotics: The new frontier in microbial biotechnology

## Summary
Prof Javier Morán of the Food Innovation Institute at UCAM in Murcia spoke on how postbiotics can reach the EU market. Morán starts from a stability problem: a probiotic product holds dead and unculturable cells as well as the live counts declared on the label. That gap, Morán says, created the postbiotic concept. Regulation, not science, then decides new launches.

Morán sets out three routes. The first is food. Morán shows the EU's recent novel-food decisions on microbes, then their own case. A sourdough bread made with lactic acid bacteria went to the German authority for a status decision. The answer was that it is not a novel food. Their study found the bacteria in the sourdough but none alive in the baked bread, and the species matched those used before 1997.

The second route is medical devices. EU device rules exclude products containing living organisms, and some notified bodies also reject heat-killed ones. Morán argues that validated inactivation shows the cells are inviable and so fit the rules.

The third route is processing aids. There a strain replaced nitrite to give sausage its cured colour. Morán closes on microbiome skin care. The regulatory picture is as of February 2025.

## Key takeaways
- Postbiotics began with a stability problem: the chart shows dead and "viable unculturable" cells growing over shelf life beside the colony-forming units declared on the label (read from the chart) (slide 2).
- Regulation is the key point for new launches, placed above science, market and marketing (slide 3).
- Food route: a sourdough bread fermented with lactic acid bacteria was ruled not a novel food after a consultation through Germany's BVL, because traditional sourdough bread was eaten to a significant degree in the EU before 15 May 1997 (slide 6).
- Their study: the sourdough held 5 x 10^7 CFU/g and the baked bread 0 CFU/g, so the bacteria are inactivated in baking while their metabolites remain; the species found matched pre-1997 literature and the EFSA QPS list (slide 8).
- Medical devices: Regulation (EU) 2017/745 defines "inviable" as incapable of metabolism or multiplication; Morán argues validated inactivation and viability tests show heat-killed microbes meet the rules, though certain notified bodies read it otherwise (slide 13).
- Processing aids: Lactobacillus fermentum at 10^8 CFU/g in place of nitrite "essentially replicated" the pigment and colour of nitrite-cured sausage (slide 16).

## For Vita Actives
- Relevance low: postbiotics sit outside our range, and the talk names none of creatine, clear pea protein or Oxystorm. It touches us only indirectly.
- My reading: the food route turns on the same test our ingredients face, a significant history of use in the EU before 15 May 1997. It is a worked example of getting a status decision from one member state (slide 6).

## Detail
- EU decisions on microbes, as the deck lists them in February 2025: Bacteroides xylanisolvens DSM 23964 approved as a novel food (2014); Mycobacterium setense manresensis approved with a use restriction (2015); Akkermansia muciniphila MucT approved with a shelf-life restriction (2021); P. freudenreichii biomass not a novel food, being consumed in fermented cheeses (2021); IBalance, 13 heat-treated microorganisms, a novel food with no history of consumption (2022); sourdough bread not a novel food, dated 2025; M. aurum Aogashima DSM33539 and Anaerobutyricum soehngenii CH106 pending (slide 5).
- The same slide says presence in the human microbiota does not guarantee safety, and that QPS listing is the gold standard; none of the six novel microbes it compares is on the QPS list (slide 5).
- The sourdough case rests on a technical dossier for the Menorca bakery S'Avia Rita, a BVL letter of 1 July 2024 and the Commission consultation result; the paper is Morán and Kilasoniya, Foods 2024, 13, 2042 (slide 6).
- Method: plate counts on MRS agar, then Oxford Nanopore sequencing, giving 13,080 reads for the sourdough, 14,575 for the dough and 14,774 for the bread (slide 8).
- Species used in sourdough before 1997 are listed country by country for Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Spain and Sweden (slide 7).
- Their conclusions table says studies confirm lactic acid bacteria improve bread digestibility and nutritional value, and that there is no evidence sourdough is used as a medicine (slide 9).
- The device argument is set out in a preprint by Morán and Kilasoniya, posted 9 July 2024 and marked not peer-reviewed (slide 12).
- Processing aids under Regulation 1333/2008 are not consumed as food in themselves and may leave only harmless, technically unavoidable residues; they may still fall under GMO, novel-food or additive rules (slide 15).
- In the sausage case, of the four strains imaged only L. fermentum shows a fluorescence signal (read from the image) (slide 16).
- The cosmetics section shows a Le Santi "microbiome skincare" range carrying a "Biotic Complex" on pack, with no text from the speaker (slide 18).
- No slide is marked "Confidential". The closing slide remembers José Luis Mendoza Pérez, 1949-2023 (slide 19).
