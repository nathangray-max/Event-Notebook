# Highlighting Gaps & Opportunities in the Microbiome-Gut-Brain Axis Field

## Summary
Dr Siobhain O'Mahony of APC Microbiome Ireland at University College Cork opened the Day 1 gut-brain session with a map of what is known and what is not. Her question: where are the gaps, and where are the openings for research and business?

She starts with the market. The human microbiome market is put at $814.6 million in 2024, rising to $4.2 billion by 2030. The gut microbiome is linked to brain development, mood, cognition and movement. But most of the strong evidence comes from animals, and how far it carries over to people is largely unclear.

She then turns to three groups. In ageing, cognitive decline goes with fewer bacteria that make butyrate. In women, the risk of dementia is higher and mood and memory change around the menopause. In athletes, about 35% of elite athletes have mental health concerns, and she sees room for microbiome products against sports anxiety.

Her close: the gut microbiome changes over life and between the sexes, and diet and lifestyle can change it. That leaves large gaps for both research and companies. Probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics or postbiotics may each be the best fit, condition by condition, and their cost-effectiveness still needs analysis.

## Key takeaways
- The animal evidence is strong but its translation is not: animal models show "profound contributions" of gut bacteria to brain activity and behaviour, yet how far these findings carry over to humans is "largely unclear" (slide 4).
- Ageing: cognitive decline goes with lower diversity and fewer short-chain-fatty-acid makers such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (less butyrate); the oldest healthy adults show more potential to make butyrate derivatives; longevity "may" also mean a more flexible, stable microbiome (slide 8).
- Women: women are at higher risk of Alzheimer's disease and dementia than men, and cognition and mood change before, during and after the menopause; 1.2 billion menopausal and post-menopausal women by 2030. The role of the microbiome in sex-specific cognition still has to be understood (slide 10).
- Athletes: about 35% of elite athletes have mental health concerns, female athletes report sports anxiety more often, and the slide names a "potential opportunity" for microbiome interventions to reduce sports anxiety and improve performance (slide 12).
- Gaps she lists: a meta-database and new algorithms, blood tests of "microbiome function", organoids, high-throughput culturing, microfluidics and AI, and condition-specific choice between probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics and postbiotics, with cost-effectiveness still to analyse (slide 14).

## Detail
- Market: human microbiome market $814.6 million in 2024 to $4.2 billion in 2030, 31.5% a year; no source is named on the slide. Figures date from February 2025 (slide 2).
- The infographic on the same slide claims "up to 90%" of all disease can be traced in some way to the gut and the microbiome, over 10,000 microbial species identified in the body, and 3.3 million non-redundant genes in the gut microbiome; humans are 99.9% alike in their genome but 80 to 90% different in their microbiome (slide 2, read from the image; attributed to Femmebiome.com).
- Her own work on the lifespan and sex slide: sex-specific gut microbiota signatures after early life stress (Neurobiology of Stress, 2024) and sex, pain and the microbiome (Brain, Behavior, and Immunity, 2022); open questions are volatility over time and hormone-driven differences (slide 3).
- Brain diseases linked to the microbiome: cerebrovascular (stroke, multiple sclerosis), neuropsychiatric (cognitive dysfunction, anxiety and depression, autism) and neurodegenerative (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS); how the microbiome plays a role "is not fully known" (Zhu et al., 2020) (slide 5).
- Brain health across the lifespan is called "a multi-billion-dollar industry"; the chart of factors places the microbiome beside healthy diet, physical exercise and cognitive activity on the protective side (Glow, 2021; read from the chart) (slide 6).
- "Global aging" raises memory loss and cognitive decline from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's disease, with "interest in identifying lifestyle-related factors" to prevent them (slide 7).
- Athletes: a review figure (Clauss et al., 2021) lists probiotic effects reported in athletes: fewer and shorter upper respiratory infections, better immune and antioxidant markers, longer run time to fatigue, better recovery after muscle-damaging exercise and lower body fat (slide 11, read from the figure).
- The Human Microbiome Compendium: over 160,000 samples from 68 nations, meant to fill gaps left by studies dominated by high-income countries (slide 13).
- Closing slide shows "the gut brain axis diet": Mediterranean-style eating, avoid processed foods, elimination diet for sensitivities, supplements and herbs "when needed" (slide 15, read from the image).
