# Highlighting Gaps & Opportunities in the Microbiome-Gut-Brain Axis Field

## Summary
Dr Siobhain O'Mahony of APC Microbiome Ireland at University College Cork opened the Day 1 gut-brain session with a map of what is known and what is not. Her question: where are the gaps, and where are the openings for research and business?

On the slides she starts with the market: the human microbiome market is put at $814.6 million in 2024, rising to $4.2 billion by 2030. In the room she began with her own field, sex and hormone differences in the gut microbiome over life. Most of the evidence comes from rodents, which she would rather call proof-of-concept tools than models of disease, and how far it carries over to people is largely unclear.

She then turned to three groups. In ageing, cognitive decline goes with fewer bacteria that make butyrate, but she does not suggest giving butyrate to everyone; prevention across life matters more. In women, dementia risk is higher and mood and memory change around the menopause. In athletes, about 35% of elite athletes have mental health concerns, sports anxiety is rising, and she sees room for microbiome products against it.

Her close: the gut microbiome changes over life and between the sexes, and diet and lifestyle can change it. That leaves large gaps for research and companies. Questions were held for the panel.

## Key takeaways
- The animal evidence is strong but its translation is not: animal models show "profound contributions" of gut bacteria to brain activity and behaviour, yet how far these carry over to humans is "largely unclear" (slide 4). Spoken, she would call rodents "proof of concept" rather than "models of disease", and said animal work is getting harder to justify in Europe [00:00].
- Ageing: cognitive decline goes with lower diversity and fewer short-chain-fatty-acid makers such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (less butyrate); longevity "may" also mean a more flexible, stable microbiome (slide 8). She is "not suggesting" butyrate for everyone as they age; lifelong prevention is the target [00:00].
- Women: higher risk of Alzheimer's disease and dementia than men; cognition and mood change before, during and after the menopause; about 1.2 billion postmenopausal women by 2030, a stage she puts at about a third of a woman's life (slide 10) [00:00].
- Athletes: about 35% of elite athletes have mental health concerns (slide 12); spoken, she put sports performance anxiety at about 35% of elite athletes [00:00]. Female athletes report it more often, and she sees "potential, possibly" for microbiome interventions to reduce it and improve performance (slide 12) [00:00].
- Gaps she lists: a meta-database and new algorithms, blood tests of "microbiome function" (accurate today but not cheap), organoids, high-throughput culturing, microfluidics and AI, and condition- and sex-specific choice of intervention, with cost-effectiveness still to analyse (slide 14) [21:19].
- On mechanism she accepts "we may never have mechanistic insights into some of this, and that's okay", as long as the links justify modulating the microbiome [00:00].

## Detail
- Market: human microbiome market $814.6 million in 2024 to $4.2 billion in 2030, 31.5% a year; no source is named on the slide. Figures date from February 2025 (slide 2).
- The infographic on the same slide claims "up to 90%" of all disease can be traced in some way to the gut and the microbiome, over 10,000 microbial species identified in the body, and 3.3 million non-redundant genes in the gut microbiome; humans are 99.9% alike in their genome but 80 to 90% different in their microbiome (slide 2, read from the image; attributed to Femmebiome.com).
- Her own work on the lifespan and sex slide: sex-specific gut microbiota signatures after early life stress (Neurobiology of Stress, 2024) and sex, pain and the microbiome (Brain, Behavior, and Immunity, 2022); open questions are volatility over time and hormone-driven differences (slide 3).
- Brain diseases linked to the microbiome: cerebrovascular (stroke, multiple sclerosis), neuropsychiatric (cognitive dysfunction, anxiety and depression, autism) and neurodegenerative (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, ALS); how the microbiome plays a role "is not fully known" (Zhu et al., 2020) (slide 5).
- Brain health across the lifespan is called "a multi-billion-dollar industry"; the chart of factors places the microbiome beside healthy diet, physical exercise and cognitive activity on the protective side (Glow, 2021; read from the chart) (slide 6).
- "Global aging" raises memory loss and cognitive decline from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer's disease, with "interest in identifying lifestyle-related factors" to prevent them (slide 7).
- Athletes: a review figure (Clauss et al., 2021) lists probiotic effects reported in athletes: fewer and shorter upper respiratory infections, better immune and antioxidant markers, longer run time to fatigue, better recovery after muscle-damaging exercise and lower body fat (slide 11, read from the figure).
- The Human Microbiome Compendium: over 160,000 samples from 68 nations, meant to fill gaps left by studies dominated by high-income countries (slide 13).
- Closing slide shows "the gut brain axis diet": Mediterranean-style eating, avoid processed foods, elimination diet for sensitivities, supplements and herbs "when needed" (slide 15, read from the image).
- Spoken, not on the slides: in a rat model of early life stress, sex-specific microbiota differences predicted which animals would be resilient or vulnerable; in a small human pain study there were sex differences in pain but minimal microbiota differences, and the female microbiome responded more to induced pain [00:00].
- She said the gut microbiome produces every neurotransmitter the central nervous system produces, and named three routes to the brain: the stress (hormonal) system, the immune system and the vagus nerve and spinal cord [00:00].
- A University College Cork study transferred faecal microbiota from people with Alzheimer's disease to mice and transferred some memory loss and amyloid plaques, as she described it [00:00].
- The brain stays plastic as we age; she named a new diet, a new exercise regime or a probiotic as possible inputs in mild cognitive impairment [00:00].
- Sports anxiety drivers she named: performance pressure, injury, overtraining, keeping peak physical health, and media and social presence [00:00].
- The large public database she showed is the Human Microbiome Compendium (Otter "Pentium") [00:00].
- No slide is marked "Confidential". The deck carries APC Microbiome Ireland copyright marks.
