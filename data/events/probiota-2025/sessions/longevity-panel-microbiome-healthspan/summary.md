# Longevity panel: the microbiome and healthspan

## Summary
Stephen Daniells of NutraIngredients chaired the Day 2 closing panel with three start-ups and one academic: Caitlin Hill of Myota (prebiotic fibre blends), Patrick Guye of Trilliome (upcycled plant fractions), Bruno Balen of AniBiome, and Sean Gibbons of the Institute for Systems Biology, back from their own talk.

Each first set out an approach to longevity. Hill argued that people ferment fibres so differently that single-fibre trials are flawed, so Myota uses a blend and measures inflammation, blood sugar, lipids and cognition. Guye said life expectancy has risen by about 20 years in 60 years while the share of life spent in good health has not.

The panel then rejected the idea that a younger microbiome is better. Gibbons warned against banking your own young stool and preferred raising the microbes that fall with age. Balen wanted measured end points, such as grip strength, over any ideal microbiome.

On centenarians, Gibbons said lifestyle can get you to 85 or 90 and genetics does the rest. On diet, Gibbons said high-protein diets can harm glucose control in young people but have been shown to prevent frailty in older people, with plant protein a little better than animal.

Asked for a $5 million trial, each named one: cognitive decline, windows of vulnerability, precision prebiotics, and a carnivore-diet comparison.

## Key takeaways
- Gibbons on protein: in young people, high-protein diets "does kind of promote loss of glucose control and type two diabetes. It can, at least", and shift gut fermentation towards protein by-products Gibbons called uremic or liver toxins; in older people a high-protein diet "has been shown to prevent frail" (Otter's words; frailty) [21:41].
- Gibbons added that plant-based proteins are "a little better" than animal protein, and that at older age an animal-type protein mix "seems to be maybe protective" against frailty (Otter: "Braille") [25:10] [26:08].
- Young is not automatically better: Gibbons cautioned against "baking your 20 year old poo and putting in yourself when you're 80", and preferred promoting the microbes that decline with age (Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia) over "the sledge hammer of fecal transplant" [06:35] [08:38].
- Hill (Myota): single-ingredient prebiotic trials are "quite flawed" because individuals ferment fibres very differently; Myota's blend is meant to work across microbiomes and is tested on IL-6, CRP, TNF-alpha, HbA1c, insulin sensitivity, lipids and cognition [00:10].
- Guye (Trilliome): in the past 60 years average life expectancy rose by 20 years but the share of life in good health "hasn't changed": about 50% in good health, 34 to 35% moderate and 14 to 15% bad [03:23].
- Outcomes over composition: Balen and Guye both named grip strength as a measure that matters, Guye calling hand force "one of the biggest predictors of health and age" [11:57] [27:22].

## Detail
- In the first recording ("Probiota 2"), the chair introduced Hill as from Myota and Guye as "co founder and CEO of Trillium" (Trilliome), which Guye said had been "pretty much under the radar until right now" [18:27].
- Also in that recording, Hill opened by noting that in 2022, in Copenhagen, ageing experts added the microbiome as a pillar of ageing, and that inflammation belongs alongside it [18:59].
- Hill: Myota sells direct to consumers and as an ingredient in functional products; its cognitive trial gives people over 60 a product (Otter: "brand role") with 10 grams of prebiotic fibre, against average UK intake of 18 grams, aiming at mild cognitive impairment before it progresses [02:35].
- Guye: Trilliome makes supplements that modulate the gut microbiome and, later, the skin's, from upcycled plant parts split into many fractions (polyphenols, flavonoids, small-molecule combinations); its current product "can massively boost a few species" linked to brain protection, which are obligate anaerobes hard to sell as probiotics [03:23].
- Gibbons: traits of youth are often anti-correlated with longevity, and a falling metabolic rate with age could protect against damage; banked young stool carries opportunistic pathogens that could harm someone later immunocompromised, so screened donors are better [06:35].
- Gibbons: a transplant from a 20-year-old to a 45 or 50-year-old "you'd probably be okay"; people usually lose Bifidobacterium by their 70s or 80s, and Lactobacillus [08:38].
- Balen: stool should be analysed by metabolomics rather than transplanted blind; live bacteria in transplants "don't survive", so what acts is metabolites; Balen's target is an "in silico projection of our best self" [09:17].
- Hill: we do not know the perfect microbiome; for women, putting a pre-menopausal microbiome into a post-menopausal body "would be potentially ineffective" [11:14].
- Balen said they personally eat "zero fiber" and earlier ate a lot of fibre as a vegetarian; Balen cares about systemic effects that can be measured [11:57].
- Guye: much of the evidence that a young microbiome is better is mouse transplant work; it needs experiments such as clearing the microbiome with antibiotics and restoring a defined consortium [13:06].
- Gibbons: exercise first, then a mostly plant-based diet, are the biggest levers; past 100, genetics dominates and centenarian studies risk survivor bias [15:00].
- Guye: ageing starts early and symptoms appear at 40 to 60, so the trajectory matters more than centenarians; Trilliome works with a longevity centre in Okinawa (Otter: "center longevity Okinawa") [17:19] [19:12].
- Hill: lifestyle signals up to 80 to 85 are clear (diet, exercise, social inclusion); the Blue Zones study is "slightly flawed" [18:26].
- Balen cited a Stanford study (Otter: "Dr zelenberg") in which another diet "was superior to high fiber diet"; Hill replied that its fibre arm was not personalised; Balen agreed fibre should be personalised [20:12] [20:21] [20:30].
- Gibbons: high-protein studies include Atkins-style diets, plant-only and animal-only protein, and liquid amino acid mixtures matching animal or plant protein; the animal-type mixture in excess "can drive some some issues with your metabolism" [26:08].
- The $5 million trials: Guye, about 250 people in early old age with first signs of cognitive decline, given defined strain mixes and perhaps prebiotics for one to two years, tracking inflammation, hand force and gait [27:22]; Hill, intervening in windows of vulnerability such as perimenopause and mild cognitive impairment [28:27]; Gibbons, precision prebiotic cocktails from community metabolic models to maximise engraftment of the microbes that fall with age, for which Gibbons was writing a grant [29:17]; Balen, healthy carnivore cohorts tested with interventions such as GLP-1 drugs (Otter: "GFP, one") or psychedelics [30:10].
