# Postbiotics, the gut barrier and the leaky gut

## Summary
Professor Maria Rescigno of Humanitas University spoke second, on why their group works with postbiotics. The gut keeps its microbes behind a two-layer mucus wall, the epithelium and a gut vascular barrier that their group described. Postbiotics, Rescigno argues, are small enough to cross all three, so they are "the mediators" of a probiotic's benefit.

Their postbiotics are metabolites made by fermenting a fibre with a Lactobacillus paracasei strain, in a patented process that also removes the gas. In their lab work the postbiotic, not the live bacteria, damped inflammatory signals in immune cells and in gut tissue from patients with inflammatory bowel disease. Live probiotics made that tissue worse.

Rescigno then linked a leaky gut to liver disease and, through a second barrier in the brain, to anxiety and depression. In mice, pre-treatment with the postbiotic protected the gut barrier against Salmonella. In a trial at Humanitas in women with breast cancer on a CDK4/6 inhibitor, no patient taking the postbiotic had grade 3 diarrhoea or had to cut or stop treatment in the first cycle. In mice it also made immunotherapy work better. Rescigno closed on personalised use.

## Key takeaways
- Postbiotics can cross the inner mucus layer, the epithelium and the gut vascular barrier, so Rescigno calls them the mediators of a probiotic's benefit [01:59].
- Their postbiotics are metabolites of Lactobacillus paracasei fermenting a fibre, separated from the bacteria; the patented process removes the gas that fibre can cause [01:59].
- In tissue from patients with inflammatory bowel disease, the probiotics tested worsened inflammation while the postbiotic reduced it [01:59].
- In mice, one week of a high-fat diet opened the gut vascular barrier and let bacteria reach the liver; pre-treatment with the postbiotic protected the barrier against Salmonella [01:59].
- In a Humanitas trial in women with breast cancer on a CDK4/6 inhibitor, grade 3 diarrhoea was avoided and no dose was cut or stopped while patients took the postbiotic; it returned when they stopped [01:59].

## Detail
- The mucus has an outer layer the microbes colonise and a firm inner layer they cannot cross [00:00].
- The gut vascular barrier works like the blood-brain barrier, protecting the liver and brain from large molecules and bacteria [01:59].
- Two postbiotics from the same strain with different substrates and processes; batches of each cluster together on metabolomics, which Rescigno presents as reproducible [01:59].
- Dendritic cells given Salmonella: adding the postbiotic, not the live probiotic, reduced the inflammatory cytokine IL-12 (Otter: "L 2070") [01:59].
- In tissue from patients with inflammatory bowel disease, the postbiotic moved NF-kappa B out of the cell nucleus and reduced several inflammatory cytokines [01:59].
- Rescigno says 40% of patients with inflammatory bowel disease suffer from depression in a phase of the disease (Otter: "blue phase"); their group found a choroid plexus barrier in the brain that closes when gut inflammation rises, linked to anxiety and depression, with evidence now in autism spectrum disorder [01:59].
- Mice were pre-treated for "10 days, I think it was" before Salmonella; the postbiotic kept the mucus, kept the barrier marker PV1 down and limited spread to the liver [01:59].
- The trial gave the postbiotic for a week before and through the first treatment cycle, with a comparison group without it; the number of patients is not given [01:59].
- In mice with triple-negative breast cancer on anti-PD-1, the postbiotic increased the treatment's effect, with more immune cells in the tumour and more MHC class I [01:59].
- The recording ends before questions; their answers in the panel that followed are in postbiotics-panel [18:48].
