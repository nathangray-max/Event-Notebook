# healthy aging and the human gut microbiome

## Summary
Sean Gibbons, an associate professor at the Institute for Systems Biology in Seattle, asked whether the changes in the gut that come with age are a decline to manage or a sign of healthy ageing. Their answer, from their group's published work, leans to the second.

In more than 4,500 adults, each person's gut grows more "unique" with age and Bacteroides falls. In men over 78, a more unique gut went with better health, such as faster walking, and in men aged 85 and over, high Bacteroides went with a higher risk of death over four years.

Gibbons then turned to statins. How well statins worked, and how far they disturbed blood sugar, varied with the make-up of the gut, and the pattern held in a separate European cohort. Gibbons suggests that probiotics targeting butyrate producers and Akkermansia could be useful.

Their conclusion is cautious. Some ageing changes may be protective: an 80-year-old probably does not want a 20-year-old's microbiome, so faecal transplants from young to old are premature. Targeted approaches and better frailty markers are needed. Questions were held over to the panel at the end of the session. The talk is from February 2025, so its figures may be dated.
## Key takeaways
- The gut microbiome becomes more unique with age, and uniqueness rises as Bacteroides falls (Spearman rho -0.73); seen in two Arivale datasets and American Gut (slide 5) (slide 8).
- In older men (MrOS, aged 78 to 98), higher uniqueness went with better health: high walk speed, excellent self-rated health, fewer medicines and a composite healthy group, in discovery and validation sets (read from the chart) (slide 8).
- Over a four-year follow-up of community-dwelling men aged 85 and over (N=257), high Bacteroides went with higher mortality (adjusted HR 1.90, 95% CI 1.37 to 2.64) and high uniqueness with lower mortality (HR 0.70, 0.50 to 0.99); across all ages only Bacteroides reached significance (read from the table) (slide 9).
- Statin response tracked gut make-up: higher diversity went with a weaker effect, and one Bacteroides-dominant enterotype had the largest LDL drop but also more insulin resistance; the results replicated in the European MetaCardis cohort (slide 16) (slide 18) (slide 19).
- Their suggestion, hedged: probiotics like A. muciniphila and butyrate producers like F. prausnitzii "could help mitigate" statins' metabolic side-effects and improve metabolic health with age (slide 20).
- The closing view: some ageing changes may be protective, and targeted interventions are "probably better" (slide 21); as spoken, an older microbiome may be adapted to an older body (thinner mucus layer, less food, slower transit), so young-to-old faecal transplants are "too early" with "not enough evidence" [00:00].

## Detail
- Two schools of thought: ageing changes in the gut as "managing the decline" (older people in assisted living) or as healthy ageing (community-dwelling people and centenarians) (slide 2).
- Cohorts: Arivale, N=3,653, aged 18 to 87, two sequencing vendors (2,539 and 1,114); MrOS, N=907 men aged 78 to 98, split into discovery (599) and validation (308); American Gut as a further check (Wilmanski et al., Nature Metabolism 2021) (slide 4).
- Uniqueness is each person's distance from their nearest neighbour; it rose step by step across age bands in both Arivale datasets and in American Gut (read from the chart) (slide 5).
- The genera most tied to uniqueness: Bacteroides and Prevotella plus Bacteroides fall (rho about -0.8 in Arivale and MrOS); several Ruminococcaceae, Christensenellaceae, Methanobrevibacter and Desulfovibrio rise (read from the chart) (slide 6).
- Uniqueness shows in the blood: microbe-derived metabolites phenylacetylglutamine, p-cresol sulfate, p-cresol glucuronide, indoleacetate and 3-indoxyl sulfate rise with it (rho up to about 0.30, read from the chart) (slide 7).
- Survival in all community-dwelling men (N=706): Bacteroides adjusted HR 1.28 (1.00 to 1.64); uniqueness HR 1.11 (0.87 to 1.42), not significant; Kaplan-Meier p=0.0026 for high against low Bacteroides (read from the table) (slide 9).
- Preliminary follow-up: uniqueness tracks a complement-inactivating C5a peptidase gene (rho 0.51), falls with a host-glycan-degrading fucosidase (rho -0.42), and rises with predicted butyrate production (r=0.53, MICOM model) (slide 10).
- Statin background: LDL response varies widely between people; in 7,856 observations some had no fall or a rise, and event rates were 11.2 per 1,000 person-years on placebo against 4.8 with a 50% or greater LDL fall (Ridiker et al., Eur Heart J 2016, as on the slide; read from the chart) (slide 12).
- Arivale statin work: 244 statin users among 1,848 with medication data; plasma HMG used as a blood marker of statin effect; it rose with dose (P=1.1e-22) and with an APOE variant linked to statin efficacy (Wilmanski et al., Med 2022) (slide 13) (slide 14) (slide 15).
- Diversity: statin users in the lowest Shannon diversity tertile had the highest HMG, that is the strongest statin effect (interaction P=0.0014) (slide 16).
- Enterotypes: LDL fell about 33% against non-users in the Bac.2 enterotype against about 16 to 20% in the others (read from the chart); Bac.1 and Bac.2 statin users also had higher HOMA-IR (P=7.2e-07 and P=1.2e-04) (slide 18).
- Implications slide: more than 2,800 people across both cohorts, 22% on statins; high Bacteroides dominance with fewer butyrate-producing clostridia and less A. muciniphila "is associated with" poorer metabolic health in statin users (slide 20).
- An extra slide after the acknowledgements: in mice, NMN supplementation raised Anaerotruncus colihominis, which in females fell as frailty rose (not significant in males); labelled "unpublished data" (read from the chart) (slide 23).
