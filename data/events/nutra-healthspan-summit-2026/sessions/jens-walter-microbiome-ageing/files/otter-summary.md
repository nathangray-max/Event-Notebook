Professor **Jens Walter** discussed the causal role of the microbiome in aging and the potential for dietary interventions to extend healthspan.

## Microbiome Aging
**Jens** highlighted research showing that the microbiome significantly influences lifespan and healthy aging.
*   **Paul O'Toole**'s 2013 **Nature** paper demonstrated that lifestyle changes alter the microbiome, with pathogens increasing and healthy bacteria decreasing as people age.
*   Animal models (mice and rats) show that removing the microbiome can extend lifespan, though calorie restriction makes the microbiome beneficial for longevity.
*   In humans, a healthy eating pattern adopted in the 40s or 50s can extend healthy lifespan by up to 10 years.
*   A decline in **Bifidobacteria**, specifically **Bifidobacterium adolescentis**, is a common marker of unhealthy aging.

## Dietary Interventions
**Jens** presented evidence regarding how specific dietary patterns modulate microbial metabolites and health outcomes.
*   **Non-industrialized type diet**: A study published in **Cell** showed that a diet high in fiber (45g) and low in saturated fat leads to:
    *   Increased **Bifidobacteria** (e.g., **B. adolescentis** and **B. longum**).
    *   Reduced detrimental, pro-inflammatory bacteria.
    *   Lower fecal pH due to increased fermentation.
    *   Higher levels of neuroprotective **indole-3-propionic acid**.
    *   Significant reductions in LDL cholesterol (17%), blood glucose, and CRP inflammation markers.
*   **Mediterranean Diet**: Intervention studies link this diet to reduced frailty and improved cognition through increased short-chain fatty acids and reduced secondary bile acids.

## Future Research
The discussion addressed several technical questions regarding the future of microbiome modulation.
*   **Probiotics vs Postbiotics**: **Jens** expressed skepticism that **postbiotics** could effectively change gut ecology or increase short-chain fatty acid production compared to **probiotics** or **symbiotics** (combining probiotics with fiber).
*   **Human Milk Oligosaccharides (HMOs)**: There is interest in whether **HMOs**, which are bifidogenic in infants, could be used to stimulate beneficial species in the elderly.
*   Could **C. elegans** serve as a viable model for establishing causality compared to rodent models?
*   Will ongoing human clinical trials for **Symbiotic Health** confirm the translatability of these microbial benefits in elderly populations?
