# Microbiome modulation through nutritional interventions to extend healthy lifespan

## Summary
Professor Jens Walter of APC Microbiome Ireland and University College Cork, founder and co-owner of Synbiotic Health, asked whether nutrition that targets the gut microbiome can extend healthy lifespan.

He started with animal studies. Germ-free rodents live longer than normal ones, but with calorie restriction the microbiome becomes beneficial, so its effect depends on diet. In humans, large observational studies link healthy eating patterns to healthy ageing; one UK study suggests sustained shifts to a healthier diet can add up to ten years, most if made in your 40s and 50s.

His own trial in Cell put healthy adults on a high-fibre, non-industrialised diet for three weeks. Bifidobacteria rose, inflammatory bacteria fell, and LDL cholesterol dropped by 16.8%. Older people rarely change their whole diet, so he sees room for alternatives: Bifidobacterium adolescentis, which falls with age and is linked with healthy ageing, paired with fibre.

He kept his hedges. Causality is clearly established in animals only, current human evidence does not prove the microbiome mediates diet's effects, and clinical trials are needed. His company's trials in older people have not reported. In questions he doubted that postbiotics can change gut ecology.

## Key takeaways
- In animal studies, rodents without a microbiome live longer. He said normal mice live about 25 months and germ-free mice almost ten months longer [00:24]. In the 1991 study on his slide, mean lifespan was 88.9 weeks germ-free against 75.9 weeks conventional on a full diet, and 117.5 weeks, the longest, for conventional mice on a restricted diet (slide 6) (slide 7).
- Observational studies link healthy dietary patterns to healthy ageing, with the Alternative Healthy Eating Index performing "consistently best" (slide 8). A UK study is titled "Life expectancy can increase by up to 10 years following sustained shifts towards healthier diets" (slide 9); he described it as ten years of healthier lifespan, and "probably" five to six years if the shift comes in the 70s [00:24].
- His Cell trial of the non-industrialised "Restore" (NiMe) diet, 45 g of fibre a day, against participants' usual diet: LDL cholesterol down 16.8% (he said 17%), glucose down 6.3%, CRP down 14.0%, BMI down 1.4%; faecal pH and the ratio of branched-chain to short-chain fatty acids also fell [00:24] (slide 14) (slide 15).
- Bifidobacterium adolescentis falls with age and is linked with healthy ageing: it peaks at about 4.8% of the gut microbiome around age 40 in healthy people (read from the chart, Synbiotic Health's data on 8,942 people), it was the strongest predictor of slower epigenetic ageing in 123 adults, and in animal studies it improved healthspan and lifespan. Synbiotic Health's human trials in older people are ongoing, with no data yet [00:24] (slide 17) (slide 20) (slide 21).
- His take-home: the gut microbiome is a plausible contributor to healthy ageing, "but human causality remains incompletely established"; complementary microbiome-targeted interventions are promising, but clinical trials are needed [00:24] (slide 22).
- He is doubtful that a postbiotic can change gut ecology or raise short-chain fatty acids, prefers probiotic bifidobacteria with fibre, and sees a possible role for postbiotics in a direct immune effect [22:01] [22:52].

## For Vita Actives
- He sees the next healthy-ageing supplements as probiotic bifidobacteria combined with fibre, because dietary change is hard to sustain in older people [00:24] (slide 22). My reading: fibre for healthy ageing may be pitched as a partner to probiotics, within what the evidence supports, which he says is not yet proven in humans.
- He showed the title of a Cell Reports paper, "Bifidobacterium adolescentis-derived nicotinic acid improves host skeletal muscle mitochondrial function to ameliorate sarcopenia" (slide 21); Otter heard "amyloid sarcopenia" [00:24]. He gave no data from it. My reading: gut-to-muscle claims may reach muscle-ageing products where CreaWin and IsoClr: Pea sit; worth watching, not borrowing.
- He cited animal research in which high-protein diets shorten lifespan, and said he is not sure how far it translates to humans [00:24]. His review figure lists plant-based protein foods among microbiome-compatible foods and red meat "in moderation" (slide 11). My reading: brands selling protein for healthy ageing may hear this; our answer should rest on human muscle evidence, and the review is about whole foods, not protein isolates.
- Postbiotics divide the speakers: Nick Stene of Euromonitor sees them leading the ingredients queuing behind NAD+, with US share up 48% and a price index of 2.83 (euromonitor-who-is-winning-healthspan); Walter doubts they can change gut ecology [22:01]; and 5% of consumers in Lumina's survey name them as most helpful for healthspan (lumina-new-longevity-consumer, slide 17). All three are given.
- Relevance medium: healthy-ageing science around fibre and probiotics; protein and muscle come up only through animal and mechanistic papers.

As he said it: co-founder, with consultancy support from companies that "do both pro and prebiotics" [00:24]. The two wordings differ; both are given.
- Paul O'Toole's work: the 2013 Nature paper "Gut microbiota composition correlates with diet and health in the elderly" (slide 3). With age, pathogens rise and healthy bacteria fall, with links to inflammation and lower barrier function; the microbiome declines further when older people move into nursing homes, probably linked to diet [00:24].
- In a literature survey (Ghosh et al., 2022), Bifidobacterium is in the group that declines with age and is linked to healthy ageing; pathobionts rise, especially in unhealthy ageing (slide 4). Observational research cannot establish cause and effect [00:24].
- Animal studies: survival curves of germ-free and conventional mice from 1966 (slide 6); read from the chart, half the conventional mice had died by about 15 to 17 months and half the germ-free mice by about 23 to 25 months. A 1991 study added food restriction: mean lifespan 75.9 weeks conventional full-fed, 117.5 conventional restricted, 88.9 germ-free full-fed, 109.6 germ-free restricted (slide 7). The finding has been repeated in mice, rats and other species; "in a lot of animal models, high protein diets actually shorten lifespan. Not sure to what degree this then translates to human." [00:24]
- Nature Medicine, "Optimal dietary patterns for healthy aging" (published 24 March 2025): the AHEI performs best; his reasons are high-quality plant foods, fat quality (PUFA, omega-3, low trans fat) and avoiding industrial risk foods such as sugary drinks, red and processed meat, sodium and trans fat (slide 8). In the talk: an unhealthy vegetarian pattern does the least good, and small amounts of wine are linked to healthier ageing [00:24].
- The UK study is Nature Food, published 20 November 2023 (slide 9). He said healthier lifespan; the paper's title says life expectancy. He added that the 40s and 50s are when to adopt the pattern [00:24].
- A systematic review in Nutrition Reviews, "Mediterranean Diet Adherence and Markers of Healthy Aging" (slide 10), published "a week or two ago" as he said it, partly from intervention studies [00:24].
- His Cell Host Microbe review of 2022, "Rethinking healthy eating in light of the gut microbiome": "Healthy Ageing Diets Are Also Microbiome-Compatible Diets!" (slide 11).
- NU-AGE (Gut, 2020): a Mediterranean diet in 612 older adults in five European countries, sampled at 0 and 12 months. It lowered bacteria associated with frailty and cognition, raised taxa linked to lower frailty, better cognition and lower CRP and IL-17, and was associated with more short-chain fatty acids and fewer secondary bile acids. The slide questions how practical the diet is for older people ("dentition, saliva, ingredients") (slide 12). He said secondary bile acids drive colon cancer [00:24]; the slide cites a Gut 2025 paper titled "Secondary bile acid production by gut bacteria promotes Western diet-associated colorectal cancer" (slide 14).
- The Cell paper: "Cardiometabolic benefits of a non-industrialized-type diet are linked to gut microbiome modulation", a randomised controlled feeding trial in healthy adults (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03501082). The Restore diet was given with a "lost microbe", Limosilactobacillus reuteri, which the talk did not mention (slide 13). He described the cohort as students and as healthy adults, three weeks on the diet, and said it was published two years ago [00:24].
- The NiMe diet: primarily plant-based; no dairy, beef or wheat; limited highly processed foods; low glycaemic index; 45 g of fibre a day (slide 15). He said the pH drop is "clearly fiber fermentation" [23:38].
- Restore diet results with p-values: BMI p=0.002, LDL p=0.003, glucose p<0.001, CRP p=0.014 (slide 15); faecal pH p=0.003 and branched-chain to short-chain fatty acids p=0.007 against no change on the usual diet (slide 14). In the talk: bifidobacteria including B. adolescentis and B. longum rose; inflammatory bacteria such as Bilophila fell; indole-3-propionic acid, a neuroprotective antioxidant with consistent benefits "specifically in mice", rose; participants lost weight [00:24].
- Possible mediators from diet to healthy ageing: saccharolytic fermentation and short-chain fatty acids; tryptophan-derived metabolites; less proteolytic fermentation and mucus degradation; fewer harmful metabolites; altered microbiome functions; ecological restructuring (slide 16).
- Bifidobacteria as fibre "amplifiers": they respond strongly to specific carbohydrates and make acetate and lactate that feed butyrate producers; B. adolescentis is adult-adapted and linked to complex carbohydrate metabolism (slide 17). Read from Synbiotic Health's charts, in unhealthy people B. adolescentis stays near 1% or below at every age (slide 17).
- The oldest living person when sampled, aged 116 as he recalled it [00:24]: Bifidobacterium at about 52% of the gut microbiome, against about 11 to 12% in 250 women and 195 men, read from the chart (Santos-Pujol et al., 2025, Cell Reports Medicine) (slide 18). He said "almost 50%" and "an N of one" [00:24].
- Sardinian centenarians (mSystems): 19 centenarians, 23 older adults and 17 young adults; read from the chart, B. adolescentis is a large share of total bifidobacteria in several centenarians (slide 19). He said it is "dramatically actually increased" in centenarians against healthy older people [00:24].
- Slower epigenetic ageing (Kunihiro et al., Scientific Reports, 2026): 123 adults, 63.4% Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander; B. adolescentis was the strongest predictor of a slower DunedinPACE. The slide's take-home: signatures "may track biological ageing pace" (slide 20).
- Mechanism papers on one slide: B. adolescentis and GABA production (Scientific Reports); his group's Microbiome paper of 2018, "Probiotic Bifidobacterium strains and galactooligosaccharides improve intestinal barrier function in obese adults but show no synergism when used together as synbiotics"; the Cell Reports sarcopenia paper; and Nature Aging (16 November 2021), B. adolescentis "improves healthspan and lifespan in multiple species" (slide 21). In the talk he gave the barrier result without the no-synergism finding [00:24].
- Lumina's earlier talk came up: he recalled that "40% of men now in the 40s think healthy eating is actually important" [00:24]. Lumina's deck shows 21% of US men in their late 40s choosing healthy eating as the single most important to long-term health goals (lumina-new-longevity-consumer, slide 14). The figures differ; both are given.
- Human milk oligosaccharides: studies show they can raise bifidobacteria in older people, but "the jury is completely out" on stimulating infant-associated species and on clinical benefit [21:22].
- Short-chain fatty acids are "very very hard to increase", even with extreme diet shifts [22:52].
- Causality: he has been on three or four papers on it; transplant models and C. elegans have limits; causal inference in humans "comes with its own bag of limitations" [23:38].
- Funders and partners include the Weston Family Foundation, Alberta Innovates, CIHR, PrecisionBiotics and the Killam Laureates; most collaborators are at the University of Alberta (slide 23). He said his interest in bifidobacteria started when he was a professor at the University of Nebraska [00:24].
