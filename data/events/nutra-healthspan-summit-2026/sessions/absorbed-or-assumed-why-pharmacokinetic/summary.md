# Absorbed or Assumed? Why Pharmacokinetic Testing Matters

## Summary
The recording holds only the last four and a half minutes of Amanda Rao's talk on pharmacokinetic testing, then one audience question. Her argument, in the part recorded: absorption studies are run on the wrong people, in the wrong conditions.

A typical pharmacokinetic study is done on a 25-year-old, while longevity products are typically sold to older women on several medications. GLP-1 medicines slow food through the gut, and studies with paracetamol show lower absorption in people taking them, so they belong in absorption studies. Studies should look at responders and at high and low absorbers, and test products as people take them: with food and coffee, with missed doses and other medications, not fasted and as a single dose.

She sees research moving from single ingredients to whole formulas, and home blood tests making it easy to track blood levels in long-term trials. AI models that predict exposure need good studies to learn from.

Asked about dose, she said a five-dose pharmacokinetic study early on is easier and cheaper than five doses in a long-term trial, and that the industry may need lower doses taken for longer.

## Key takeaways
- In the studies she cited, people on GLP-1 medicines absorbed paracetamol 15 to 50 percent less; absorption studies that leave them out are "not you know, collecting the right data" [00:00].
- Study populations do not match buyers: a typical pharmacokinetic study is on a 25-year-old, while longevity products are typically sold to 65-year-old women on three medications, with low oestrogen and a history of antibiotics [00:00].
- Look at responders and at high and low absorbers, not only at a rise in the blood: "did 90% of people get that?" [00:00].
- Test products as they are taken: most pharmacokinetic studies are fasted and single-dose, while real users eat, drink coffee, miss doses and stack medications [00:00].
- Long-term trials should measure blood levels as well as outcomes, now that home tests are easy; some nutrients, such as iron and vitamin D, need longer studies [00:00].
- On dose, a five-dose pharmacokinetic study early is "easier and honestly cheaper" than five doses in a long-term study; she suggested lower doses for longer [05:14].
