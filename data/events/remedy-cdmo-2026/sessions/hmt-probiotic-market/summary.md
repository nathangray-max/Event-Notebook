# Beyond Probiotics: Where the Biotics Category Is Really Heading

## Summary
Marit Weinstra of HMT, the Healthy Marketing Team, a food and health branding and innovation agency, opened the probiotic programme. Her question was how to win in a biotics market that has grown into a jungle of products. Use keeps rising, yet in seven of eight European countries consumers do not feel informed about probiotics.

She traced four eras. Yakult made good bacteria a daily ritual. Activia made the benefit tangible, and brands began competing on CFU counts. Then survival technology became the story. Now the shift is from adding bacteria to nourishing the gut ecosystem, with the gut barrier as the gateway to the rest of the body.

From that she drew four rules: quality and proof over "more is better", feeding the ecosystem, benefits consumers can measure and feel, and everyday food formats. Her examples were mostly American: Pendulum's Akkermansia products for metabolic health, a prebiotic fibre brand, a digestive brand that sets expectations week by week, and Poppi.

Her conclusion: in Europe most of these claims cannot be made, so the brand has to carry them. Value is moving from what is inside the product to what the consumer gets. A strong probiotic needs a "Double IP": the science and the brand.

## Key takeaways
- Biotics have never been more popular: probiotic use among US adults rose 287% from 2009 to 2023, and European online probiotic sales have grown 15-25% a year since 2020 (slide 2). Yet in 7 of 8 European countries consumers do not feel informed about probiotics (slide 6); she said this without figures [00:24].
- Rule 1, from "more is better" to quality and proof: a number on its own no longer builds trust, so be an expert brand in a niche (slide 14). Shelves show 50, 80 or 100 billion CFU [03:59]; her niche example is Pendulum, an Akkermansia-based US brand for metabolic health (slide 14) [03:59].
- Rule 2, from adding bacteria to feeding the ecosystem: "eat more plants" instead of strain names, plants and fibre consumers can see, and a gut they feed themselves (slide 15); fibre brands lead this language [03:59].
- Rule 3, from belief to measurable and felt benefits: early adopters measure with microbiome tests, apps and personal scores; the mass market wants less bloating, regularity and energy, and buys again only if it notices a difference (slide 16). Her example sets expectations at week 1 and months 1, 2 and 3 (slide 17); she said "for one week, for one month" [03:59].
- Rule 4, from supplement to everyday food and formats: products designed for the person, not the bacteria; "the best biotic is the one people actually take every day" (slide 18). Poppi is her example [03:59].
- In Europe you cannot claim most of it, so the brand carries it: BioGaia's baby drops say "for colic & digestive discomfort" in the US but "one of the world's most researched lactic acid bacteria strains" in Europe (slide 20), and Activia names its strain, Bifidus ActiRegularis (slide 21) [03:59].

## For Vita Actives
- Relevance stays medium: nothing in the talk or slides covers protein, creatine or sports nutrition, but the branding argument transfers to our customers' categories.
- The question has changed from "what's in it?" to "what does it do in me?" (slide 11). My reading: the same shift faces sports and active nutrition brands, and it favours ingredients whose effect a user notices; that is how a CreaWin or IsoClr: Pea pitch should be framed.
- Activia's branded strain on its ingredient list and BioGaia's research claim show how a European brand carries what it cannot claim (slides 20-21). My reading: an argument for branded ingredients, and for getting the CreaWin and IsoClr: Pea names onto customers' packs with a story told within claim rules.
- HMT lists B2B2C communication among its services, with Suntheanine as the case (slide 24). My reading: HMT is an agency, not a buyer; it could brand our ingredients to end consumers or support a customer's launch.

- Rule 2's pictured example is LOAM Prebiotic Fiber Formula (slide 15); she added that the language has moved from one prebiotic fibre to plant diversity [03:59].
- Rule 3's example is the be levels digestive product, whose own claims shown on the slide are 90% less bloating and gas, 92% an overall improvement in digestion and 89% more regular bowel movements; its timeline runs from lighter digestion in week 1 to balanced digestion by month 3 (slide 17). She said this framing is "maybe not so science-based" but easier for consumers to understand [03:59].
- Rule 4: Poppi, "not because it's a good quality product necessarily, but because they really turned it into a format that people like" [03:59]; Poppi cans are pictured (slide 18).
- The Double IP: rational IP is intellectual property (the science, the innovation); emotional IP is intellectual positioning (the story, the brand), called the "HMT formula for transformation" (slide 22). She said the brand gives longer-term protection and value [03:59].
