# Remember it. Make it. Prove it. Three things your brief forgot to ask

## Summary
Thomas Beadnall, presenting for Solabia Nutrition, opened with the brief every brand writes and every CDMO receives: clinically proven, clean label, cost-effective, innovative, ready in Q3, two-year shelf life. He called it impossible and replaced it with three questions: will they remember it, can you make it, can you prove it.

On memory, the name decides. MicrobiomeX, a citrus flavanol with a prebiotic effect, went nowhere as a "flavobiotic" and was repositioned as the co-pilot for your probiotic. An aronia berry extract named Brainberry is now Solabia's second biggest and fastest growing product.

On making it, briefs now ask for several grams of creatine and collagen plus botanicals in one soluble format. Creatine adds its own problem: brands want it in gummies, but heat turns it into creatinine. Solabia and the Polish CDMO Tantus avoided this with a cold-pressed tablet of creatine and Solabia's citrus extract WATTS'UP.

On proof, he said "science-backed" means nothing on its own. What counts is the dose, the population, the endpoints and the study design. Solabia has run randomised placebo-controlled trials on all its ingredients and put each study on its website, with the olive leaf extract Bonolive as his example. He closed by inviting brands to bring their briefs to Solabia.

## Key takeaways
- The standard brief is "impossible"; judge a launch on whether they will remember it, whether you can make it and whether you can prove it [00:01] (slide 2) (slide 4).
- Naming decides memory: "Flavobiotic" did not fly and MicrobiomeX became "The co-pilot for your probiotic", while Brainberry, an aronia extract with the benefit in its name, is now Solabia's second biggest and fastest growing product [00:01] (slide 7) (slide 8) (slide 10).
- Briefs now ask for three to five grams of creatine and five to ten grams of collagen plus a botanical extract and a vitamin complex in one format that is soluble and mixes well [00:01]. The slides show these only as pictures, with no figures (slide 12).
- Creatine in gummies: heat applied over time gives creatinine, which "doesn't really do much"; Tantus's answer was a cold-pressed tablet, no heat, of creatine plus WATTS'UP [00:01] (slide 13) (slide 14).
- "Science-backed" means nothing on its own. The slide's open-evidence test is dose (how much?), population (tested on whom?), endpoints (what changed?) and study design (how was it tested?); he also named the change against placebo [00:01] (slide 16) (slide 17).

## For Vita Actives

The heat problem in gummies is an opening for a stable creatine and for formats that avoid heat. My reading: a CreaWin pitch to CDMOs should carry stability data for cold-pressed and chewable formats.
- Tantus (Poland) is a CDMO already making a creatine chewable with Solabia's WATTS'UP [00:01]; neither deck names the creatine's source (tantus-longevity-concepts, slide 7). - A benefit in the name beat a mechanism word [00:01] (slide 7) (slide 10). My reading: relevant to how we name concepts for customers.
