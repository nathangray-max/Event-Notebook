# Translating Longevity Science into Relevant Consumer Outcomes

## Summary
Alex Glover, Holland & Barrett's Global Product Formulation Lead, runs the team that formulates its own-label products worldwide. He asked how longevity science becomes something a customer will buy. His answer: consumers may buy a story, but they judge a product on the outcomes they feel.

He opened with a tortoise to argue that ageing badly is not biologically fixed, so the category is about living better, not longer. H&B's data on millions of customers show that repeat purchase goes to products with one clearly communicated benefit, such as joint function or brain function. Shoppers ask when they will feel it, whether they can measure it, and what it costs. His slides set the test an ingredient must pass: evidence, dose, outcome, communication and consumer relevance.

Two case studies followed. NAD boosters have a strong molecular rationale but very limited functional outcome data, and of over 1000 customers surveyed, not one knew what NAD was. Curcumin, H&B's second best-selling ingredient, shows the other path: a meta-analysis of 11 trials found it improved knee osteoarthritis pain scores. He also named cheap basics with strong evidence: fibre, vitamin D, calcium and protein.

His conclusion for suppliers: bring ingredients with both a biological rationale and human outcome data, and help retailers translate the science without over-promising.

## Key takeaways
- Mechanisms make promises and sell, but outcomes retain customers (slide 6). Shoppers ask whether and when they will feel it, whether they can measure it, and what it costs (slide 8); when trials show a benefit at four, six or eight weeks, H&B can sometimes put that on pack [00:25].
- H&B's test for an ingredient has five parts: evidence (high-quality science), dose (the effective, studied dose), outcome (which endpoint improves), communication (legal and responsible) and consumer relevance (does it matter in everyday life) (slide 6).
- The outcomes consumers buy are energy, memory, strength, mobility, sleep, appearance and feeling younger or better, not NAD+, sirtuins, telomeres or PARPs (slide 8). H&B's biggest own-label sellers carry one clear benefit, such as joint function, joint comfort or brain function [00:25].
- NAD: of over 1000 customers surveyed, not one knew what NAD was [00:25]. NR reliably raises blood NAD metabolites and NMN or NAM raise it in some studies, but functional outcome improvements are "very limited" (slide 13).
- Curcumin is the "tangible outcomes" case: a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (n=1258) found curcuminoids improved VAS and WOMAC pain scores in knee osteoarthritis against comparators or placebo, and formulation matters because bioavailability varies widely (slide 15).
- Cheap interventions with strong evidence (fibre, vitamin D, calcium, protein) beat novel technologies on evidence and cost; US shelves are "protein plus fiber" [00:25]. None of this is on his slides.

## Detail
- The deck names him Alex Glover MSc, RNutr, Global Product Formulation Lead, Holland & Barrett (slide 1). Every slide carries the date 04/11/2026; none is marked confidential (slide 2).
- Agenda in five parts: from lifespan to healthspan; consumers don't buy longevity, they buy outcomes; the longevity evidence gap; NAD, NR and NMN as a "molecular outcomes" case study; joints as a "tangible outcomes" case study (slide 2). He skipped it in the talk [00:25].
- The tortoise: a photo, then a survival chart labelled with an average lifespan of about 72-80 years for humans and 150-200 years for the tortoise (slides 3-4). He said humans live about 10 to 12 years with a disability or poor health, while a tortoise lives most of its life without [00:25]. In his words, "we're not in the business of making people live longer; we're making them live better" [00:25].
- H&B's data on millions of customers show what they buy and how often. A customer panel, the "Stay Healthies" around 50, talked about playing with grandchildren and getting out of a chair, not sirtuins or telomeres [00:25].
- The evidence chain on his slide runs mechanism (pathway), biomarker (surrogate), function (performance), clinical outcome (quality of life, disease endpoint) (slide 9). He said suppliers get "stuck" in the first two; his sleep example was the Pittsburgh scale, which means nothing to a shopper [00:25].
- Biomarkers are real signals but often not validated surrogates; a biomarker change is not a change in how someone feels or functions; in supplements, biomarker shifts often precede but do not guarantee outcome benefits (slide 10).
- NAD: a central redox cofactor that declines with age, behind the hypothesis "boost NAD = boost healthspan"; blood NAD is not muscle or mitochondrial NAD, and more in blood does not guarantee more where it matters; measurement is improving but interpretation is still hard (slide 12). NR can raise the muscle NAD metabolome, with mixed outcomes; NMN and NAM outcomes are mixed or early (slide 13).
- The NAD paper: he spoke of an April 2026 meta-analysis and one trial on walking speed in the over-60s [00:25]. The slide shows a different source: a review published 13 October 2025, "NAD+ precursor supplementation in human ageing: clinical evidence and challenges" (Vinten et al., Nature Metabolism 7, 1974-1990), beside three trials: chronic NR in healthy middle-aged and older adults (Martens et al.), NR in aged human skeletal muscle (Elhassan et al., 2019), and a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled NMN trial in healthy middle-aged adults (published 8 December 2022) (slide 13). The spoken date and the slide differ; the walking-speed trial is not on the slide.
- Curcumin is H&B's second best-selling ingredient globally, about "one bottle a minute"; he gave the on-hold claim as joint flexibility and comfort, with evidence on knee osteoarthritis, movement, function and systemic inflammation [00:25]. The slide cites Hsiao et al., a meta-analysis of high- and low-dose curcumin in knee osteoarthritis, and a critical review of curcumin reviews (Chen et al.): adverse events were similar at high and low dose, and curcumin sometimes compares favourably with NSAIDs (slide 15). Systemic inflammation is not on the slide.
- His conclusions: the most compelling retail categories are those where consumers can feel or measure the outcome (mobility, cognition, vision); curcumin, UC-II and anthocyanins show mechanism aligning with real-world outcomes; NAD boosters show strong rationale but limited evidence for tangible benefits "so far"; the retailer's challenge is to translate emerging science without over-promising (slide 17).
- Fibre, vitamin D, calcium and protein: he said the nutrition slide was missing from the deck [00:25]. He gave about 9% lower coronary heart disease risk for every seven grams of fibre, called fibre one of the biggest nutritional predictors of longevity, and costed vitamin D at about two cents per 10 microgram serving from H&B's own label [00:25].
- He believes about 80% of how we age can be influenced. He closed on an 81-year-old sprinter he named as Kenton Brown, who ran 100 metres in 14.4 seconds [00:25]; the slide shows the sprinter in a USA vest without a name (slide 16).
- The talk was one unbroken Otter turn, so every spoken passage sits in the [00:25] block.
