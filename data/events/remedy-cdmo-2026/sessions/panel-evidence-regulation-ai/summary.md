# Closing panel: evidence, regulation, supply and AI in nutraceuticals

## Summary
The day's closing panel put a retailer (Alex Glover, Holland & Barrett), a CDMO (Martina), an ingredient developer (Matt), a food lawyer (Joanna) and a pharmaceutical company (Alexandra) in front of a moderator who asked what each wants in the next two or three years, then turned to regulation, supply and AI. Wants: evidence-based claims and better communication to consumers (Alexandra [02:10]); a "revolution" in clinical trials, with high-dose studies in people with real problems, published so that consumers' chatbots find credible sources (Matt [02:52], [03:45]); a focus on what consumers actually need (Martina [04:30]); and food law as "a shield" for innovators, since the claims system was written for vitamins (Joanna [05:36]). Alex said educated consumers want evidence but not the EFSA wording, that brands should do a few claims well, and that H&B starts with the consumer problem and invests in trials and branded ingredients where it wants to win [08:33]. Matt said a new brain ingredient takes three years longer to approve in Europe than in the US, which "almost makes the innovation impossible" [14:53], and warned of a fish oil supply collapse already hitting aquaculture that reaches shelves next summer [18:48]; Alex described nine months of pre-bought stock and protecting the claim the consumer buys [19:36]; Joanna said "my supplier's fault" is no defence [22:09]. On AI the panel wanted a human in the loop (Joanna [23:33]), peer-reviewed databases (Alexandra [24:59]), and, from Matt, a ChatGPT over factory sensors and "if you can't operate AI... you can't work two years from now" [25:42].

## Key takeaways
- Matt: trials in this industry study healthy people for short periods, so effects look small; he would run high-dose studies in people with real problems and publish, because consumers will find it through chatbots [02:52].
- Matt: AI assistants rank credibility, peer-reviewed science and university pages, after LinkedIn and Reddit were gamed; the language in published studies matters [03:45], [03:59].
- Joanna: EU food law and the claims regulation were written for vitamin C and D; innovators must combine what is legally possible with showing what trials found; food law should be "a shield", not "a heartbreak" [05:36].
- Alex: consumers want evidence but not the EFSA wording; "throwing the kitchen sink" (90 plus ingredients, 35 claims) muddles the message; do a few things well for a defined audience [08:33].
- Alex: H&B's own label has "customer missions" (joint, bone and muscle care; skin; weight management); where it wants to win it runs primary research with universities, clinical trials and standardised or branded ingredients; the lesson of seven years is start with the consumer problem [08:33].
- Martina: legislation is an obstacle for a CDMO; do not fill every gap, stick to one piece and explain it; the customer, not the CDMO, has the power to sell [11:13], [12:26].
- Alexandra: "what is on the box should be in the box during the shelf life"; brand means trust and quality [13:33].
- Matt: same safety package, three years longer approval in Europe than the US; cost today, income later, so the delay "almost makes the innovation impossible"; micro-influencer social selling arriving from China via the US is "almost impossible to regulate" [14:53].
- Matt: fish oil supply is collapsing for aquaculture now and reaches supplement shelves next summer; aquaculture buys the alternatives with higher willingness to pay; the risks are losing consumers to other ways of managing health, and shortcuts where traceability is absent [18:48], [21:19].
- Alex: H&B holds up to nine months of pre-purchased stock on core lines; shoppers give three or four seconds at shelf, so protect the claim they buy (heart, eye) and find the alternative; rhodiola's endangered listing brought about 400 customer emails [19:36].
- Joanna: liability stays with you; share responsibility by contract; "it's my supplier's fault" is not a defence [22:09].
- AI: a human in the loop for anything legally binding and for IP (Joanna [23:33]); peer-reviewed databases, not blogs (Alexandra [24:59]); a ChatGPT over factory sensors, and "if you can't operate AI, you have you can't work two years from now" (Matt [25:42]).
- Panellists' own supplements: Alex takes creatine daily, omega-3, astaxanthin, vitamin D in winter and pre-workout; Matt protein, creatine, magnesium, zinc, vitamin D; Martina magnesium and krill oil [00:30], [00:51], [00:57].
- Closing asks: speak the consumer's language (Alex [30:07]); bring lawyers in at the laboratory stage (Joanna [27:27], [28:09]); talk to people outside your company (Matt [28:55]); think what you want, not what the market says (Martina [29:34]).

## For Vita Actives
- Two of five panellists take creatine daily, including H&B's formulation lead [00:30], [00:57]; my reading: creatine is now a default daily supplement for industry insiders, which supports CreaWin's move into everyday and healthy-ageing products beyond sports.
- A retailer and a CDMO both say the brief is the consumer problem and a few claims done well [08:33], [11:13]; my reading: our customer-facing materials should give one outcome per ingredient with the trial that shows it, not a list.
- The fish oil shortage [18:48] is not ours, but H&B's response, protect the benefit and find the alternative, shows how a retailer treats any supply shock; my reading: supply continuity and traceability for CreaWin and IsoClr: Pea are selling points to put in front of buyers now.
- Europe's three-year approval lag [14:53] means novel protein and creatine formats will launch in the US first (my reading).
- Chatbots rank peer-reviewed science [03:45]; my reading: publishing the CreaWin and IsoClr: Pea evidence where AI assistants can find it is now part of marketing.
- Relevance medium: regulation, supply and retail views, adjacent to our ingredients.
