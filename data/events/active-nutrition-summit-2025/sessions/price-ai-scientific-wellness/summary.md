# AI, Scientific Wellness and the Future of Personalised Health

## Summary
Nathan Price, Chief Scientific Officer of Thorne and a professor at the Buck Institute, argued that AI lets personalised nutrition use very deep data on one person and still give that person simple advice.

His evidence comes from scientific wellness work on about 5,000 people, each measured for their genome, about 1,200 blood analytes, gut microbiome and self-tracking. First, genetics. A polygenic score explains about 12% of LDL cholesterol. People whose measured level sat well above their genetic prediction lowered it in a lifestyle programme; people whose genes predicted a high level did not. He calls that gap the "Modifiability Gap", a guide to where advice is most likely to work.

Second, the microbiome. Blood metabolites predict about half the variation in gut diversity. The microbiome's gene content was the only measure that predicted weight loss once BMI was accounted for. And some gut bacteria turn phosphatidylcholine into TMAO, so a supplement taken for the brain could harm the heart in those people.

He called for trials that test a personalisation algorithm rather than one nutrient at a time. His conclusion: AI powered by deep phenotyping will transform personalised nutrition, and genetics are not destiny.

## Key takeaways
- Genetics predicted who lowered LDL cholesterol in a personalised programme: those measured well above their genetic prediction came down "very substantially"; those predicted high showed no significant change [00:00] (slide 11).
- The "Modifiability Gap" between genetic prediction and measured value marks the people "most likely to respond"; he would rank these gaps per person to plan advice that visibly works and keeps them engaged [00:00] (slide 12).
- In the cohort, the gene content of the gut microbiome was the only predictor of weight loss independent of BMI, a correlation of about 0.3; blood metabolites, proteins and a diet questionnaire showed no signal beyond BMI [00:00] (slide 18).
- Gut bacteria in some people convert phosphatidylcholine to trimethylamine, which the liver turns into TMAO, a cardiovascular risk factor; a population trial cannot show who this applies to [00:00] (slide 16).
- He wants randomised trials that test the personalisation algorithm, so each person's supplement stack differs while one thing is still tested rigorously [00:00].
- The microbiome becomes more unique to each person from around age 50; his "uniqueness" score predicted all-cause mortality in people over about 80 over a four-year follow-up [00:00] (slide 17).

## Detail
- The deck is marked "Proprietary & Confidential" on its title slide (slide 1). Its title slide reads "AI, Scientific Wellness, and the Future of Personalized Nutrition" (slide 1).
- He founded Arivale about 10 years ago; it generated much of the data shown and went out of business [00:00].
- His team has a $52 million US grant, part of which builds a "personalized health span optimizer", using large language models and agentic AI to move from pattern recognition to "deep reason" on one individual [00:00].
- Deloitte (2022): most of the $4 trillion spent on US healthcare goes on treating sickness; Deloitte forecasts a large shift towards wellness and prevention by 2040, which he called the "$2 trillion pivot" [00:00] (slide 5).
- On Alzheimer's he contrasted a drug costing $20,000 to $40,000 a year for a couple of months' delay with prevention giving "years, sometimes many years"; he said 45% of dementia in the US Hispanic population is preventable by lifestyle and nutrition [00:00].
- Pioneer 5000 (PIs Nathan Price and Lee Hood; Price et al, Nature Biotechnology, 2017): whole-genome sequencing; lab tests three times (clinical chemistry 150, metabolites 700, proteins 400, about 1,200 analytes); gut microbiome three times; continual self-tracking; a database of 200 actionable possibilities (slide 6) (slide 7).
- Genetics alone captures about 12% of the variance in LDL cholesterol, enough to move someone across the clinical range (Zubair et al, Scientific Reports, 2019) [00:00] (slide 10). He said the same holds for raising HDL cholesterol and appears to for a marker of the transition to diabetes [00:00].
- About half the variance in gut microbiome diversity can be predicted from blood metabolites, from as few as 11; the link held across conditions on the slide (abdominal pain, acid reflux, antibiotics, bloating, constipation, diarrhoea, type 2 diabetes) but broke down in extreme obesity, BMI over about 35, which he thinks is due to inflammation (Wilmanski et al, Nature Biotechnology, 2019) [00:00] (slide 15).
- About 18% of circulating metabolites are made or co-made by the microbiome [00:00]. The slide names indole propionate, TMAO, imidazole propionate, hippurate, secondary bile acids and p-cresol sulfate as nutrients and toxins with effects in the host (slide 16).
- Weight loss: people whose microbiome breaks complex carbohydrate into simple sugars "have a really hard time losing weight"; those whose microbiome turns it more into short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate lost more in a personalised programme (Diener et al, mSystems, 2021) [00:00] (slide 18).
- Biological BMI: traditional BMI "Misclassifies up to 30% of people"; a metric built from metabolites, clinical labs and proteins "May identify metabolically unhealthy individuals who occupy a normal BMI" (Watanabe et al, Nature Medicine, 2023) (slide 19).
- He ran his own comprehensive blood panel through ChatGPT's deep research function and called the result "probably the most informative health report I've ever gotten" [00:00].
- In questions, asked about Rob Knight's view (heard as "Rob night") that the microbiome may be destiny, he said "it is an influence", not destiny, and that understanding the genome "gives you a roadmap" to better choices [26:54].
- He referred to an earlier talk by a speaker heard as "Mary" and "Marion" [00:00]. My reading: probably Mariette Abrahams (abrahams-glp1-ai-personalised-nutrition), recorded the same day.
