# Athlete Microbiome

## Summary
Paul Cotter, Head of Food Biosciences at Teagasc and a principal investigator at APC Microbiome Ireland, presented what studies of elite athletes' gut microbiomes have taught their group, and what it means for diet.

Cotter started with the gut microbiome and the many factors that shape it, from antibiotics and birth mode to diet, fibre, fermented foods, probiotics and exercise. Their group's first study compared elite rugby players with men of low and high body mass index. The athletes' microbiomes differed from both control groups, though with exceptions, and later work showed differences in what the microbes can do and the metabolites they make, including short-chain fatty acids.

Cotter then turned to diet. In a 12-week trial in 53 healthy adults, a ketogenic diet lost slightly more fat than a low-sugar diet but raised LDL cholesterol, reduced glucose tolerance and lowered bifidobacteria; cutting sugar changed the microbiome little. Cotter also showed kefirs that differed in their effect on plasma cholesterol, and work separating the effects of diet and exercise on gut microbe and host metabolism.

The deck ends with Sportgenix, a microbiome analysis programme for athletes linked to SeqBiome, the company where Cotter is CTO.

## Key takeaways
- Elite rugby players' microbiomes differed from those of low- and high-BMI controls, "though there are exceptions" (Clarke et al., Gut 2014; 40 athletes, 23 and 23 controls, men aged 18 to 37) (slide 7) (slide 8).
- Athletes' microbiomes also differ in functional potential and in the metabolites they produce; short-chain fatty acids correlate with diet or microbial diversity (Barton et al., Gut 2018) (slide 9).
- Ketogenic diet, 12 weeks in 53 healthy adults: 2.9 kg average fat loss against 2.1 kg on a low-sugar diet, but keto raised LDL cholesterol, reduced glucose tolerance and decreased bifidobacteria; sugar restriction did not significantly change the microbiome (slide 10) (slide 11).
- Fermented foods can differ in effect: kefirs differed in their impact on total plasma cholesterol, under the heading "Fermented foods with anti-obesity impacts" (Bourrie et al., Br J Nutr 2021) (slide 12).
- Diet and exercise each shape microbe and host co-metabolism, scored from urine NMR profiles against WHO healthy-eating guidelines (Penney et al., mSystems 2020) (slide 14) (slide 15).

## For Vita Actives
- Relevance medium: gut health in sport is a science story, not yet a product story; the deck names no supplement ingredient or dose. My reading: useful background for fibre and prebiotic pitches to sports brands, within each ingredient's own evidence.

## Detail
- Their roles: Head of Food Biosciences, Teagasc Food Research Centre, Moorepark; Principal Investigator, APC Microbiome Ireland and VistaMilk; CTO, SeqBiome Ltd (slide 1).
- A microbiome-in-numbers infographic opens the science (Sender et al., Cell 2016) (slide 2).
- Gut microbiota functions listed include vitamin synthesis, satiety, digestion and absorption, immune stimulation, energy harvest from food and signalling to the brain; these are "Dictated by types of microbes, what they do/produce and microbial diversity" (slide 3). Microbial products such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, TMA and LPS act on the heart, liver, muscle and brain (Shehata et al., Exp Physiol 2022) (slide 4).
- Factors that shape the microbiome: antibiotics, stress, birth mode, breast feeding, environment, probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, postbiotics, diet (including fibre and fermented foods) and exercise, with the consensus definitions of the four biotics (slide 5).
- Exercise influences the gut microbiota in adults, with links shown to depression and anxiety, infection, gastrointestinal permeability and inflammation (Wosinska et al., Nutrients 2019) (slide 6).
- The rugby study measured fasting blood markers, clinical bloods, diet (food frequency questionnaire), body composition (DXA and waist-to-hip) and faecal sequencing (slide 7).
- The keto trial is cited on the slide as Hengist et al., 2024, Cell Reports Medicine (slide 10): control moderate-sugar diet; low-sugar under 5% of calories from sugar; keto under 8% from carbohydrate; neither changed physical activity. The low-sugar diet "significantly reduced cholesterol in LDL particles" (slide 10).
- Kefir chart: total plasma cholesterol for five samples labelled COM, ICK, Pitch, PNY and PNL, with ICK and Pitch significantly lower than the others, read from the chart; the codes and the study population are not explained on the slide (slide 12).
- Sportgenix (sportgenix.ie) is shown as a personalised microbiome analysis programme for elite athlete performance, beside the SeqBiome logo, read from the slide images (slide 6) (slide 12).
- Collaborators acknowledged at Teagasc, UCC, the Irish Sports Institute, Imperial College London and the University of Bath, and "All athletes; elite and non" (slide 13).
