# Nutrition by Algorithm

## Summary
Alice Millbank, co-founder and chief scientific officer of Rooted Research Collective, asked what goes wrong when people take their nutrition advice from social media algorithms rather than from dietary guidelines.

Her starting point is that guidelines are evidence-based but rarely followed, while social media reaches far more people: one pro-carnivore post drew 3.7 million likes, against 82 million engagements for a two-year UK COVID campaign. She then presented her group's study of 53 nutrition "super-spreaders" with a combined reach of over 24 million, sorted into three personas: the Doc (borrowed authority), the Rebel (counter-culture) and the Hustler (quick-fix wellness). Most had no health qualifications and nearly all had a financial incentive. Their themes were carnivore and keto diets, anti-seed-oil messages, distrust of institutions, and the demonisation of "fake meats", legumes, grains and milk alternatives.

Her conclusion for industry: this noise shapes how consumers see functional ingredients and the whole sector. Brands should simplify without denying complexity, avoid silver-bullet promises, tell the truth, use storytelling ethically and check the values and credibility of the influencers they pay.

## Key takeaways
- 57% of Gen Z and Millennials are influenced by TikTok nutrition content, and "only 2.1% aligned with public health guidelines", in the slide's words (slide 9).
- One pro-carnivore post drew 3.7 million likes; a two-year UK COVID social media campaign drew 82 million post engagements (slide 10).
- Of 53 super-spreader profiles, 59% had no formal health qualifications, only 7 were verified medical doctors, and 96% had clear financial incentives, many selling their own supplements or programmes (slide 14) (slide 18) (slide 19).
- Doc accounts had the most followers on average (744,950), against 400,877 for Hustlers and 362,533 for Rebels; Hustlers had the highest average engagement, 43.5% (slide 15) (slide 17) (slide 18).
- The spread runs on fear-mongering, joy-mongering and "sprinkling" misinformation into non-food content, framed as us versus them, shared frustrations, storytelling, and "natural" and "ancestral" as moral frames (slide 20) (slide 21).
- For industry: misinformation influences behaviour and trust, "disenchants people from functional ingredients" and colours perception of the whole industry (slide 24).

## Detail
- Slide numbers are the PDF's pages (26). The opening slide shows a salt lamp sold for "ONLY £59.98" (slide 2); it carries no other text.
- Talk outline: who she is, the importance of nutrition, where people go for advice, the rise of misinformation, understanding its spread, what industry can and should do (slide 4).
- Rooted Research Collective works across food, sustainability, health and international development: digging into data, uncovering facts, challenging misinformation (slide 5).
- The study was published as "Nutrition Misinformation in the Digital Age: 2024-2025"; the slide shows press coverage from May 2025 in Food Ingredients First, New Food, ConfectioneryNews, vegconomist, Green Queen, Plant Based News and VegNews. One clipping describes it as a joint analysis with the Freedom Food Alliance, read from the image (slide 6).
- Why nutrition matters: it influences maternal health, child well-being, cognition and economic productivity; overnutrition now outweighs undernutrition globally; diet is the leading preventable risk factor for non-communicable diseases (slide 7).
- An estimated 0.1% of UK adults follow the Eatwell Guide, and many UK children fall short of basic nutrition standards (slide 8).
- Personas: the Doc, 12 profiles, average engagement 37.7%, mostly fear-mongering, paid through coaching and business; the Rebel, 15 profiles, 39.9%, fear- and joy-mongering, business and affiliate income; the Hustler, 26 profiles, 43.5%, joy-mongering and sprinkling, affiliate and sponsorship income. The example posts on these slides are marked "not real posts" (slide 15) (slide 16) (slide 17).
- Misuse of authority: only 1 internist among the 7 verified MDs, and 5 of 53 held non-medical doctorates while prominently displaying "Dr" (slide 18).
- Money: one Doc-profile super-spreader "reportedly earns $100k+ per month from social media alone"; unqualified health influencers charge $100 to 250 an hour for consultations, some selling biohacking programmes worth tens of thousands (slide 19).
- Prominent themes: carnivore, keto, anti-seed oil; tallow and raw milk "promoted as silver bullet solutions"; distrust in institutions and health professionals; "natural" and "ancestral" living (slide 12).
- Consequences: in the WHO European Region 59% of adults and almost 1 in 3 children are overweight or obese; extreme, exclusionary diets; "simple solutionism" that reduces nutrition to silver bullets; tips that contradict global guidance and fuel distrust (slide 23).
- She set the UK government's dietary recommendations and the Eatwell Guide against a post titled "How I lost weight in 3 simple steps" (slide 22).
