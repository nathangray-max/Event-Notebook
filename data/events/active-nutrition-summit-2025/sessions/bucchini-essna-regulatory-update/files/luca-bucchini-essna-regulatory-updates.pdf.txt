ESSNA’s objectives
❖ Removing regulatory barriers and promoting a positive policy
environment for sports nutrition products and active consumers.

Maximum amounts of vitamins and minerals
7
➢ The European Commission (EC) is planning to harmonise
maximum amounts for vitamins and minerals in food
supplements and fortified foods.
➢ While concrete details have not been made public and may
change, very restrictive EU-wide maximum amounts may be
introduced.
➢ For example, addition of a vitamin like B6 or potassium to
drinks might not be allowed with no claims permitted, or
sodium might not be allowed in electrolyte drinks without
added sugars.

Maximum amounts of vitamins and minerals
8
➢ Such low amounts may result in some sports nutrition products
losing their functionality and ability to make authorised health
claims crucial for the sector, and not only in one-off
reformulation costs.
➢ At the same time, harmonisation would be important for our
industry. If this effort fails, France (and potentially Germany)
appear poised to propose even more restrictive maximum
amounts
➢ ESSNA is actively engaging with EU Member States and the
European Commission to ensure that the specificities of sports
nutrition are considered when setting these amounts.

Nutrition and Health claims
9
➢ The NHCR regulation makes it difficult to communicate
the benefits of sports nutrition products effectively.
➢ Valid claims have been rejected and “on hold” claims
on botanicals are causing market fragmentation.
Despite the aims of the regulation, sports nutrition
research is not benefiting.
➢ For example, new probiotics for sports nutrition have
been researched, but benefits cannot be
communicated.
➢ A more flexible approach for structure-function claims
for sports benefits would be required.
23.6.2025

Novel foods Regulation
➢ The interpretation of the novel food regulation has expanded its scope
significantly.
➢ The approval process is expensive, lengthy and complex making
Europe less attractive for new ingredients and slowing the pace of
innovation.
➢ The EFSA performance review might offer an opportunity to streamline
a process to support innovation while ensuring consumer safety.

Ultra-Processed Foods
➢ There is growing concern around ultra processed foods.
➢ The definition of UPF is not consistent. Sports nutrition products may fit
the definition at least from a formal standpoint.
➢ Policymakers need to recognise the wide variety of processed foods
and adopt a science-based, harmonised and non-discriminatory
approach in any future legislation to help consumers make informed
choices.
➢ ESSNA is monitoring all proposals and specifically definitions.

Social Media promoting illegal substances to GenZ
➢ More and more influencers are
promoting and explaining steroid use,
making it seem normal and accessible.
Reports suggest increased use.
➢ Social media algorithms amplify this
trend, influencing young people to
prioritize quick results over health.
➢ The sports nutrition industry needs to
explain and affirm what safe sports
nutrition is, and educate the younger
generations.
➢ ESSNA is working with authorities and
police forces to raise awareness.

Concluding remarks
➢ After many years of regulatory certainty, the sports and active nutrition industry
faces a new wave of regulatory concerns, and challenge from the illegal unsafe
products.
➢ Harmonisation of maximum amounts must take the specificities of the industry
into consideration to ensure that they functionality and claims are not lost.
➢ The health claims and novel food approval processes need to be streamlined
for us to remain competitive. Ultra-Processed Foods should not be our concern,
but we are following discussions with full attention.
➢ Normalisation of doping and of other illegal substances for GenZ requires
extraordinary education and communication efforts.
