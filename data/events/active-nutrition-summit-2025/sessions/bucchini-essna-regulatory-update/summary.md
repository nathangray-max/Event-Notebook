# Regulatory Updates, Challenges & Opportunities in Active Nutrition

## Summary
Luca Bucchini, Chair of ESSNA, the European trade association for sports and active nutrition, set out the regulatory issues facing the sector in Europe.

The biggest is maximum amounts of vitamins and minerals. Limits differ between member states today, so a product legal in Belgium may not be legal in Italy. The European Commission has restarted work to harmonise them, and a leaked working document suggested very restrictive limits: sodium might not be allowed in electrolyte drinks without added sugars, and vitamin B6 or potassium might not be allowed in drinks. He said the real cost is lost functionality and lost health claims, not one-off reformulation. Yet harmonisation matters: if it fails, France and perhaps Germany may set even tighter limits.

He then covered health claims, where approvals have dried up and botanical claims remain on hold; novel foods, where the scope has grown and approval is slow and costly; the Green Claims Directive; ultra-processed foods, whose definition could catch sports products; and influencers normalising steroids for Gen Z.

His conclusion: after many years of regulatory certainty, the sector faces a new wave of concern. If the maximum levels are set right, they could open markets.

## Key takeaways
- The European Commission plans EU-wide maximum amounts for vitamins and minerals in supplements and fortified foods; details "may change", but "very restrictive" limits may come, for example no sodium in electrolyte drinks without added sugars [00:00] (slide 7).
- He said the real loss would be functionality and the ability to make authorised health claims, not one-off reformulation costs [00:00] (slide 8).
- Harmonisation still matters: if it fails, "France (and potentially Germany)" appear poised to propose even more restrictive maximum amounts [00:00] (slide 8).
- Health claims: valid claims have been rejected, "on hold" botanical claims fragment the market, approvals have stopped and applications have fallen to zero; new sports probiotics cannot communicate their benefits [00:00] (slide 9).
- Novel foods: the regulation's scope has expanded and approval is "expensive, lengthy and complex"; the EFSA performance review may be a chance to streamline it [00:00] (slide 10).
- Influencers are making steroid use look normal to Gen Z; ESSNA is working with authorities, Europol included, and calls on the industry to explain what safe sports nutrition is [00:00] [14:58] (slide 13).

## Detail
- ESSNA was created in 2003 "to prevent the introduction of EU legislation which could have been damaging to the industry"; it represents the whole supply chain to national and European policy-makers (slide 4). Its objectives include EU-UK regulatory divergence and "Tackling inadvertent doping, non-compliance and unscrupulous online vendors" (slide 5).
- His example of today's patchwork: a 2 g vitamin C capsule, one a day, cannot be sold in France or Italy [00:00]. The Commission first tried to harmonise about five years ago, and has restarted; the work covers supplements and foods such as protein bars and drinks [00:00].
- He heard of the leaked working document through a member's call about sodium a few months before; ESSNA is engaging member states and the Commission so sports nutrition's needs are considered [00:00] (slide 8).
- The claims system was meant to reward companies that spend millions on trials; he showed a chart of rejected and approved claims and said approvals stopped long ago and applicants have lost trust [00:00]. The slide asks for "A more flexible approach for structure-function claims for sports benefits" (slide 9).
- Novel food: an officer from a German-speaking country told him a mango smoothie nobody had made before 1997 would be a novel food [00:00].
- Green Claims Directive: the slide says European institutions are negotiating the final text (slide 11); in the talk he said the Commission had decided to pull the proposal after a political backlash [00:00]. The two differ; the deck predates the talk.
- Ultra-processed foods: the definition is not consistent and sports nutrition products may fit it "at least from a formal standpoint"; reviews are under way in the UK and EU, and ESSNA is monitoring the definitions (slide 12). He said the mechanism behind the epidemiology is not known [00:00].
- Social media: he told Europol's annual meeting about the problem; brands are being built on steroids and other substances, injectable and in pills, and a German customs officer linked seized products to an influencer-promoted brand [00:00] [14:58] (slide 13).
