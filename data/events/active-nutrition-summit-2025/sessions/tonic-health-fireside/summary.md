# Fireside with Tonic Health

## Summary
Sunna Van Kampen, founder and CEO of Tonic Health, a UK supplement brand, talked with Olivia Haslam of NutraIngredients about building a brand almost entirely on social media.

Van Kampen started the business during COVID after finding that most supermarket supplements were dosed too low to correct deficiencies. Their products use what they called the right doses, without sugar, sweeteners or additives. Science-heavy podcasts and newsletters did not work: focus groups showed customers had no time for them. What did work was short videos, under 60 seconds, telling people to swap one product for another and why. After 10 to 15 TikTok posts, one reached 250,000 views; the brand now gets 50 million views a month.

Their case is that consumers buy supplements on trust and awareness, so click-through rates per post miss the point; they credit the brand's growth entirely to social media. They never make medical claims, but argue the ASA holds back science, and that regulators should focus on safety and fake products, such as a creatine brand found to contain no creatine.

Next come recipe content and a podcast with Channel Four.

## Key takeaways
- The brand's origin: most supermarket supplements were "such low dose" that they did little; Tonic set out to give "the right doses" without sugar, sweeteners and additives [01:11].
- Science content did not reach consumers; under-60-second "swap this for this" videos did, starting on TikTok [02:43] [04:41].
- They do not promote their own products on social media: it is now entertainment media, where the best content wins regardless of followers [05:22].
- Supplements are bought on trust and awareness, so per-post sales tracking is "a big waste of time"; a team of six grew the brand on social media alone [06:42].
- Regulation should target safety and fraud: they cited 75% of mushroom brands on Amazon not containing the mushroom, and a creatine brand with about 10 million in sales and no creatine in it [13:06].

## Detail
- The first viral video came after "maybe 1015 posts" (10 to 15) on TikTok, with about 250,000 views; they were early to TikTok as social media moved from pictures to video [04:41].
- They said social media is now the number one source of news globally, bigger than TV, and the only channel that lets a brand grow without millions to spend on TV [08:11].
- Risks: a competitor (Otter heard "fuel") paid influencers for take-down posts of the brand; audiences call out formulations and untrue claims [08:11].
- Regulation: other supplement websites link products to autism and ADHD without data; they never make medical claims but will "push the ASA" where science is ahead of the rules, and asked "Who is the ASA?" They called UK health "a mess" [10:53].
- The mushroom figure refers to an earlier talk that morning (nordic-mushrooms-functional-mushrooms) [13:06].
- They argued that factory farming and ultra-processed food reduce the nutrition people get, so more supplements will be needed [14:07].
- Next: recipe content showing how easy it is to eat whole foods, and a podcast launching "next week, Friday" with Channel Four, with celebrity guests; an earlier podcast failed for lack of distribution [14:07] [17:13].
