# Creatine for Lifelong Nutrition & Healthy Ageing

## Summary
Sergej Ostojic, Professor of Nutrition and Public Health at the University of Agder in Norway, argued that creatine should be treated as a semi-essential nutrient for everyone, not only as a sports supplement.

The body turns over about 2 g of creatine a day and makes about half; the rest has to come from food, almost all of it meat and fish. Older people make less and eat less, and ageing muscle may take it up less well. In US survey data, 65.2% of adults ate less than the 1 g a day Ostojic takes as the requirement.

Ostojic then showed population studies linking low dietary creatine with more depression, heart and liver conditions, poorer cognition, cancer and earlier death, and higher creatine intake with more lean mass and grip strength. These are associations; some weakened once other factors were adjusted for. Ostojic set them beside supplementation trials in older people and the EU-authorised claim for adults over 55 who do resistance training, and called creatine safe on more than 1,000 human studies.

Their advice for older people: 1 g a day for life to prevent a shortfall, 3 to 5 g with exercise for function, and more for the brain. Ostojic wants creatine recognised as a semi-essential nutrient and added to everyday meat-free foods.

## Key takeaways
- Their frame: "Creatine appears to be a semi-essential nutrient for humans". About half of the 2 g daily turnover comes from an omnivorous diet, and older people make less, eat less and may take up less into muscle (slide 8) (slide 10) (slide 23) [00:00].
- Intake is low and falling: 65.2% of NHANES adults ate less than 1.00 g a day, and dietary creatine fell significantly in infants and in children and adolescents, with a tendency in adults, across 89,161 respondents (slide 11).
- Low intake is associated with worse health. Depression prevalence was 42% higher in the lowest intake quartile than the highest; older people under 1 g a day had 2.62 times the risk of angina pectoris and 2.59 times the risk of liver conditions (slide 12) (slide 13). Ostojic presented these as associations from population studies.
- Higher intake is associated with more lean mass and grip strength without exercise, in children, adults and older people; Ostojic said the link weakens and is not always significant once age and sex are adjusted for (slide 16) [00:00].
- The authorised claim: "Daily creatine consumption can enhance the effect of resistance training on muscle strength in adults over the age of 55" (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/672) (slide 17).
- Doses for older people: 1 g a day for life to prevent a shortfall; 3 to 5 g a day with exercise to raise stores and performance; up to 20 g a day for cognition, for 5 to 7 days and then 3 to 5 g (slide 19).

## Detail
- Disclosures: member of the Scientific Advisory Board on Creatine in Health and Medicine (AlzChem LLC); co-owner of a European patent on liquid creatine supplements (WO2019150323 A1) and a US patent application on creatine for telomere lengthening; research support in the past 36 months from AlzChem GmbH, Kaneka Nutrients, ThermoLife International, Vireo Systems, a science ministry and a provincial secretariat for research; co-founder of KRE-ALL (slide 2).
- 100 years of research: a 1926 paper already suggested "Creatine may be stored as a reserve food material" (slide 4); a PubMed search shows 69,774 results (slide 5).
- Roles beyond energy: neurotransmitter, antioxidant, buffer and appetite regulator; muscle holds 120 mmol/kg of dry mass (slide 6). Ostojic said a just-completed study suggests creatine may affect appetite, submitted the week before [00:00].
- Food sources, in g/kg: herring 6.5 to 10.0, pork 5.0, beef and salmon 4.5, cod 3.0, tuna 4.0, plaice 2.0, milk 0.1; fruit, vegetables and shrimp only trace (slide 8).
- Groups at risk of a shortfall: preterm infants, inherited metabolic disorders, myopathies, multiple sclerosis, sarcopenia, vegetarians, chronic kidney disease and older people (slide 9).
- Why older people run low: less made by the body (lower AGAT and GAMT activity), less eaten, less muscle, and possibly a less effective creatine transporter (SLC6A8), which "is still under investigation" (slide 10).
- Cognition: older people eating more than 0.95 g a day (the top two quartiles) scored higher on cognitive tests (slide 13).
- Cancer: "for every additional milligram of food creatine per kilogram of body mass consumed daily, the cancer rate is reduced by approximately one percent", in US adults aged 20 and over; the slide calls this an association (slide 14).
- Mortality, from a paper in revision: survival was higher in those meeting the recommended intake (P 0.05). Hazard ratios: crude 0.85 (0.62 to 1.00); 1.03 (0.85 to 1.24) after sociodemographic factors; 0.96 (0.77 to 1.19) after lifestyle; 0.79 (0.67 to 0.94, P 0.01) after diet; 0.81 (0.69 to 0.96) after physical examination; 0.92 (0.72 to 1.17) after laboratory measures (slide 15). Ostojic spoke of 20 years of follow-up [00:00].
- Lean mass and grip, from a paper in review: lean mass excluding bone 43.5, 43.9, 46.8 and 50.2, and handgrip 58.9, 57.7, 63.9 and 71.1, from the lowest to the highest intake quartile (up to 0.21 g a day to 1.04 g or more) (slide 16). In people 65 and over the crude coefficients were 2.67 for lean mass and 3.68 for grip, both significant; in the most adjusted model shown for them, lean mass stayed significant (3.12) and grip did not (1.11) (slide 16). Ostojic spoke of 130,000 US adults [00:00].
- Supplementation in older people, as listed: less liver fat, better glycaemic control, slower tumour growth in some cancers, more strength or muscle, less bone loss, better function in knee osteoarthritis, better cognition, an antidepressant effect (slide 17). Ostojic said more than 50 studies in older people in the past 10 years [00:00].
- Safety: no risk in cross-sectional studies; no severe adverse events in long-term trials; over 1,000 human interventional studies at 0.3 to 0.8 g/kg a day for 7 days to 5 years; minor risks from RCTs: weight gain from water (?), kidney problems (?) and passing gut upset (slide 18). Ostojic said stomach upset may occur in the first 10 days [00:00].
- Open questions: no RDA or upper limit for older people; individual needs by sex, illness and microbiota; food composition data; who is at risk; interactions with other foods and medicines (slide 20).
- Fortification: eating less meat for the climate may lower creatine intake; adding creatine to meat-free foods could prevent a large-scale shortfall; first voluntary, market-driven fortification, then possibly mandated fortification (slide 21). Ostojic suggested a company could make a creatine product for older people [00:00].
- Their group in Norway built the Creatine Global Initiative (creatine.global), a library bringing together industry, scientists, regulators and consumers (slide 22) [00:00].
- Q&A: asked whether current production is environmentally friendly, Ostojic said most companies use synthetic processes, which are environmentally friendly compared with the old extraction from meat [23:44].
